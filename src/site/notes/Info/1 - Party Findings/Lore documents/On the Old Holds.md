# On the Old Holds

_By Brother Aspar of the Brotherhood of Kyne, written circa 2E 172_

## Preface

_On the Memory of the Nords_

In the one hundred and seventy-second year of the Second Era, when the snows fell early upon the Jerall and the crows gathered thick upon the roofs of Windhelm, I came into possession of several weathered chronicles concerning the eastern holds of Skyrim. These were not the proud annals of Cyrodilic historians, nor the neat temple records favored by the scribes of High Rock, but rather fragments: skaldic lays half remembered, cracked stone tablets taken from barrows, and the testimonies of grey-bearded hunters who swore by Kyne that what they told me had once been sung in the halls of kings.

From these scraps I have endeavored to fashion this humble work.

Let the reader understand the outset that the Nords are a people who keep their history not only in ink, but in song, stone, and bone. A tale carved upon a barrow door may be older than a city, and a drinking song shouted over mead in a Windhelm hall may preserve truth long forgotten by scholars. Yet these same tales are also prone to smelling like a sail in a strong wind. A jarl becomes a king, a battle becomes a war, and a draugr in a mound becomes a demon of the end-times if enough winters pass between the telling.

Thus the histories of Skyrim must be approached as one approaches a deep forest: carefully, with patience, and with knowledge that the path beneath one's feet may well have been laid by ghosts.

I, Brother Aspar of the Brotherhood of Kyne, am no skald by birth, though I have drunk beside many and borrowed freely from their tongues. Nor am I a court historian in the manner of the Imperials, who arrange the past as a mason arranges bricks, each fitted squarely atop the other until a wall is made. My vows bind me instead to Kyne, Mother of Storms, whose breath gave the Nords their first voice upon the Throat of the World. It is perhaps fitting, then, that I record the stories the as they have come to me: scattered upon the wind, yet part of the same storm.

The subject of this book is what many Nords call the Old Holds: the eastern realms of Eastmarch, The Pale, The Rift, and Winterhold.

These lands claim a greater antiquity than their western kin. Their jalrs often boast that their thrones descend in unbroken line from the companions of Ysgramor, who first led the Nords from Atmora into Skyrim's frozen embrace. Whether this is truth or merely the pride of old families I leave to wise heads to judge. Yet none can deny that the east is thick with the marks of ancient days: long barrows filled with draugr-kings, crumbling watchtowers whose builders are forgotten, and standing stones older than the oldest songs.

In these holds the past walks close beside the living.

A farmer in the Pale may plow a field whose stones were once the walls of a dragon cult fortress. A fisher of Winterhold ight swear that the sea speaks in the voices of drowned jarls when the wind turns north. And in the Rift, beneath the red canopy of aspen trees, it is said that some paths still follow the very roads first trod by the Five Hundred Companions.

Such matters are easily dismissed by those who have never wintered in Skyrim. But I have seen enough to know that the Nords are not wring in believe their dead linger near.

Whether this book records history, legend, or some mingling of the two, I leave to the judgement of the reader. I have written what I have found, and what I have heard, and what I myself have witness in my travels among the eastern holds.

If there are errors within these pages, and surely there must be, then I pray that Kyne forgive my ignorance, and that future scholars correct me with kinder pens that those of the Cyrodiils, who are known to sharpen theirs like daggers.

Let it be remembered only that these lands are old, and that memory itself grows strange with age.

And so I set quill to parchment to speak of the Old Holds of Skyrim, whose stones remember more than any man now living.

-- Brother Aspar,
Servant of Kyne,
Written in the 172nd year of the Second Era,
in a wind-beaten hall outside Riften.

## Chapter I

**_On the Nature of the Old Holds_**

Those who travel Skyrim from west to east will tell you that the land itself changes character as one rides. The mountains grow sharper, the wind harsher, and the people less inclined to welcome a stranger's questions. Some claim this is merely the temperament of the eastern jarls, who are said to prize tradition above comfort and suspicion above hospitality.

Yet the Nords themselves offer a simpler explanation.

"The east remembers," they say.

By this they mean not only that the land is old, though it is certainly that, but that the memory of Skyrim's earliest days lingers more strongly there than anywhere else in the province.

When Ysgramor first returned from exile in Atmora, bringing with him the Five Hundred Companions to reclaim the lands lost after the Night of Tears, it was along the northern and eastern coasts that his people first secured their foothold. The harbors were harsh but defensible, the forests thick with game, and the mountains rich with stone and iron. In those days the west was a distant frontier, and many of the valleys beyond the reach were still contested by elves, spirits, and stranger things whose names are now spoken only in barrow-whispers.

Thus the earliest Nords kingdoms grew first in the east.

The cities that endured today, Windhelm, WInterhold, and the lesser seats that dot the coasts and valleys, claim ancestry from those first settlements. Their jarls often boast that their halls stand upon the bones of the earliest longhouses raised by the Companions themselves. Whether tis is truth or merely the fond exaggeration of proud lineages, the antiquity of the eastern holds cannot be denied.

Even the earth there seems thick with history.

In Eastmarch, ancient barrows rise like sleeping beasts among the hot springs and frozen marshes. Many bear carvings whose runes have worn so smooth that even the cleverest priests of Skyrim struggle to name them. Some say these tombs were raised in the days of the Dragon Priests, when the dragons ruled openly over the Nords and demanded worship as living gods. Others claim they are older still, built by chieftains whose names vanished when the first kingdoms rose.

The truth may lie buried beneath many layers of stone.

Far to the north lies The Pale, a land whose bleak hills and wind-scoured plains give the hold its somber name. Few regions of Skyrim possess so many burial mounds. Travelers often remark that the land seems less inhabited by the living than watched over by the dead. A farmer may uncover a stone crypt with his plow as easily as he might strike a buried root. 

It is perhaps no surprise that the people of the Pale speak often of ghosts.

Southward, beyond the high mountain passes, spreads The Rift, whose red forests and fertile valleys make it seem almost gentle when compared with the harsher lands of the north. Yet this appearance deceives. Beneath those amber leaves lie ruins as ancient as any in Skyrim, and the hills conceal tombs where kings who ruled before the founding of the great cities are said to sleep. The Nords of the Rift are fond of reminding travelers that while their land may appear softer, its history is no less bloody.

And then there is Winterhold, perched precariously upon the northern cliffs where the Sea of Ghosts gnaws endlessly at the stone. Among the Old Holds it is perhaps the strangest. The city has long been known as a seat of learning and magic, attracting scholars, priests, and hedge-wizards from distant lands. Yet the Nords themselves remain wary of such pursuits, and many whisper that Winterhold’s fondness for arcane knowledge is the very reason the sea watches it so hungrily.

Indeed, more than one old fisherman has told me that the waves beneath those cliffs carry grudges older than kingdoms.

Together these four lands form what the Nords call the Old Holds. The name does not merely speak to their age, though that alone would suffice. Rather, it reflects the belief—held strongly among their jarls—that these regions preserve the truest traditions of the Nord people.

In the west, where trade with High Rock and Cyrodiil is more common, customs have softened with the passage of centuries. Foreign coin flows through their markets, and foreign ideas sometimes find welcome in their halls.

But in the east the old ways cling stubbornly to life.

The jarls there still recite their lineage as though the Companions might return tomorrow to challenge them for their thrones. Mead-halls resound with sagas whose origins are lost to time. And the ancient barrows—silent though they may appear—stand as reminders that the Nords have never entirely left their past behind.

Whether this is a strength or a burden depends greatly upon whom one asks.

Some say the Old Holds preserve the true heart of Skyrim. Others argue that they cling too tightly to the ghosts of an age long gone. I will not pretend to judge between these views. I merely record that the eastern holds are older, sterner, and more haunted by memory than the rest of the province.

And perhaps that is reason enough for their name.

For the holds of the east are not merely old.

They are the Old Holds.

## Chapter II: Eastmarch, the Hearth of Kings

If the Olds Holds may be called the memory of Skyrim, then Eastmarch is its beating heart.

Few lands in the north are so steeped in the sagas of the Nords. Here the earth steams with the breath of hidden fires, the marshes glitter with frost beneath long winters, and ancient barrows rise from the soil like the knuckles of some great buried giant. In Eastmarch, it is said that one cannot walk a mile without stepping upon the shadow of the past.

At the center of this land stands Windhelm, the oldest city of men in Skyrim and, according to many of its proud citizens, the oldest in all of Tamriel. Its black stone walls loom high above the White River, their age betrayed only by the countless winters that have gnawed at their edges. Tradition holds that the city was founded by Ysgramor himself upon his return from Atmora, when he led the Five Hundred Companions to reclaim the land from the elves.

From this stronghold the first kings of the Nords are said to have ruled the great northern empire that once stretched far beyond the borders of modern Skyrim. The skalds of Windhelm sing proudly of those days, when Nord longships sailed the coasts of distant lands and the banners of the dragon-kings flew over conquered cities.

How much of this empire existed as the sagas describe it, I cannot say. The Nords themselves remember such matters with great enthusiasm and only modest concern for exact numbers. Yet there is little doubt that Windhelm once stood at the center of a realm greater than the jarldoms we know today.

Even now the city bears the weight of that legacy. Its jarls speak often of ancient kings, and its warriors carry themselves with a pride that seems older than the stones beneath their feet.

Many claim that the spirits of the The Companions themselves still walk the halls of Windhelm on winter nights, though these tales may owe more to strong mead than to reliable observation.

---

Beyond the walls of Windhelm the land of Eastmarch spreads wide and untamed.

Along its river valleys and wooded hills stand the ruins of ancient settlements and burial mounds raised during the earliest centuries of Nord rule. Some are little more than collapsed stones swallowed by moss and time. Others remain disturbingly intact, as though their builders had only recently abandoned them.

Among these ruins is Ansilvund, whose broken towers rise from a lonely hill overlooking the eastern road. The Nords claim it was once the seat of a proud clan that sought to command the dead through forbidden rites. The tale ends, as such stories often do, with the clan destroyed and their barrows cursed. Travelers are advised not to linger there after sunset, though I note that such warnings rarely deter the bold—or the foolish.

Farther north lies Cronvangr, a ruin half-swallowed by the surrounding forest. Its stones are thick with claw marks and other signs that the creatures of the wild have claimed the place as their own. Yet beneath the shattered halls lie older crypts whose runes suggest they were raised during the earliest centuries after the return from Atmora.

Some of these carvings depict warriors kneeling before shapes that may represent dragons.

Whether these images record ancient worship or merely the artistic enthusiasm of their makers is difficult to say.

Hidden deeper in the woods is Sivdur's Respite, a small and easily overlooked barrow said to house the remains of a minor chieftain who fought beside the early kings of Windhelm. The tomb itself is unremarkable when compared with the grander barrows of Skyrim, yet its name appears in several fragmentary sagas concerning battles long forgotten.

The dead of Eastmarch are numerous enough that even its lesser tombs possess stories.

Of greater renown is Yngol Barrow, which stands upon the northern coast where the Sea of Ghosts gnaws ceaselessly at the land. According to the sagas, this mound holds the remains of Yngol, the son of Ysgramor, who was lost during the earliest voyages from Atmora. The barrow’s interior is said to be haunted by strange lights that drift through the air like wandering stars.

Many claim these lights are the restless spirits of those who drowned during the great migration.

Others insist they are merely tricks of wind and frost.

I confess that I did not remain long enough to form my own conclusion.

---

Not all the ruins of Eastmarch belong to the Nords.

Deep within the mountains to the west lie the shattered remains of Mzulft, one of the ancient cities of the Dwemer, the vanished Deep Elves whose mastery of metal and steam remains unmatched in Tamriel. Much of the city lies buried beneath rockfalls and centuries of neglect, yet the portions that remain visible hint at a vast complex of halls and towers that once burrowed deep into the mountain.

The Nords seldom approach these ruins willingly.

They claim that the machines of the Dwemer still stir in the dark places below, and that the metal guardians of that forgotten people have not entirely abandoned their posts. Whether such tales are true or merely convenient excuses for avoiding dangerous tunnels, I cannot say.

Yet the few scholars who have attempted to explore Mzulft speak of strange lights in the depths and corridors that hum with unseen power.

If the Dwemer left any legacy behind them, it sleeps uneasily there.

---

Closer to Windhelm stands Morvunskar, an old fortress perched upon a rocky rise. To the casual eye it appears little more than a crumbling ruin, yet beneath its foundations lie tunnels and crypts that many believe once served as the burial place of early kings.

Several local traditions insist that the fortress was raised to guard the royal tombs of Windhelm during the first centuries of the Nord kingdoms. If so, the structure has long since fallen from that duty. The stones are cracked, the gates broken, and the surrounding hills are known to shelter all manner of unsavory folk.

Still, those who explore the lower passages speak of sealed chambers and ancient sarcophagi whose inscriptions have been deliberately chiseled away.

Why such names were erased, none can say.

---

Not all places of power in Eastmarch belong to the dead.

South of Windhelm, where the hot springs rise from the earth in clouds of white steam, lies the village of Kyne's Grove. To passing travelers it appears little different from other rural settlements: a scattering of wooden halls, smoke rising from hearth fires, and the distant rumble of the nearby volcanic hills.

Yet among the Nords this place holds a reputation that borders on reverence.

For generations the grove has been home to a quiet sisterhood devoted to Kyne, whom the Nords honor as the mother of storms and the breath of the world. These women—along with a handful of men who share their calling—practice what the locals refer to as the Clever Craft: a mixture of old Nord magic, herbal lore, and spiritual rites said to descend from the earliest days of the people.

The practitioners themselves are often called witches by outsiders, though the people of Eastmarch rarely speak the word with fear. In times of sickness, drought, or ill fortune, it is to Kyne’s Grove that many farmers and hunters quietly travel.

The sisters ask little payment for their aid beyond offerings to Kyne and respect for the old ways.

Some scholars dismiss these practices as rustic superstition. Yet I have seen wounds healed there that would have slain a man elsewhere, and storms predicted with uncanny precision.

If the Clever Craft is merely superstition, it is at least a remarkably useful one.

---

Thus Eastmarch remains a land where the ancient world lingers close to the surface.

Its capital remembers the age of kings.
Its barrows house the dead of forgotten wars.
Its mountains conceal the works of vanished peoples.
And in the steaming valleys of Kyne’s Grove, the old spirits of Skyrim are still honored as they were in the earliest sagas.

It is little wonder, then, that the Nords of Eastmarch often speak as though the past has never truly ended.

In that land, one sometimes suspects they may be right.

## Chapter III: The Pale, Where the Dead Keep Watch

Of all the Old Holds, none bears a name more fitting than The Pale.

At first glance the land appears simple enough: a wide tundra of pale grasses and low hills stretching between the mountains and the Sea of Ghosts. Yet the longer one travels there, the more one begins to understand why the Nords speak of the hold with a peculiar mixture of pride and unease.

The soil is thin, the winds relentless, and the winter lingers long after other lands of Skyrim have begun to thaw. Even in summer the land retains a faded, colorless cast, as though the sun itself tires of shining upon it.

The Nords say the land is pale because the dead lie close beneath it.

Whether this is truth or merely poetry, I cannot say. Yet nowhere in Skyrim have I seen so many burial mounds scattered across the hills as I have in the Pale.

---

Upon the northern edge of this bleak country stands Dawnstar, a harbor town clinging stubbornly to the cliffs of the Sea of Ghosts. The settlement is small by the standards of the great cities, yet its importance far exceeds its modest size. For many generations it has served as one of the principal northern ports of Skyrim, linking the Old Holds to distant coasts through the icy trade routes known among sailors as the Whale Roads.

Dawnstar is not a gentle harbor.

The sea there is treacherous, and its winds have dashed more ships against the rocks than any harbor-master would care to count. Even on calm days the water carries a chill that bites through wool and fur alike. Yet the fishermen of the town sail out regardless, their boats cutting through grey waves in search of horker, cod, and the great northern whales whose oil burns bright in the halls of jarls.

Visitors often remark upon the grim humor of Dawnstar’s people. Perhaps it is only natural for those who live so close to the Sea of Ghosts, whose name was earned long before the first Nord ship ever crossed its waters.

On clear nights the auroras above the harbor shine so brightly that the sea itself seems filled with pale fire.

More than one old sailor has sworn that the lights sometimes move against the wind.

---

South of Dawnstar the land rises gently into the basin of Lake Yorgrim, one of the few places in the Pale where the land softens enough to support true settlement. The lake lies cradled between low hills, its dark waters fed by streams descending from the surrounding tundra.

In winter the lake freezes thick enough for wagons to cross its surface, though wise travelers prefer the safety of the roads.

The Nords of the region tell several conflicting tales regarding the lake’s name. Some claim it honors an ancient chieftain called Yorgrim who drowned there during a winter hunt. Others insist the lake was named for a war-leader who vanished beneath its waters after pursuing a wounded ice-wraith across the ice.

There are even those who whisper that something older than the Nords sleeps beneath the lake’s frozen depths.

I leave such matters to the imagination of the reader.

---

Near the southern edge of the lake’s basin stands the small settlement of Dunstad, a town whose existence is owed entirely to the stubbornness of its inhabitants. The land there is marginal at best for farming, and the winds that sweep down from the northern coast rarely show mercy to crops.

Yet the people of Dunstad endure.

They herd hardy goats across the tundra, trap fox and hare in the surrounding hills, and trade what little they produce with the merchants of Dawnstar. Their houses are low and thick-walled, built to resist the gales that sweep across the plains with little warning.

In my brief stay among them I found the people wary but not unfriendly. They are accustomed to travelers passing through, though they rarely understand why anyone would choose to do so in the first place.

For the road between Dunstad and Dawnstar is not a pleasant one.

---

Between the harbor and the inland town stretch the windswept plains known as the Stovelrod Steppes. Few regions of Skyrim appear so deceptively empty.

The land there rolls gently beneath a wide sky, its grasses pale and brittle beneath the northern winds. At a distance the steppe seems harmless enough, yet travelers soon discover that its dangers lie not in steep cliffs or deep forests, but in its very openness.

Storms sweep across the plains with alarming speed. A clear sky can darken within minutes, burying the land beneath blinding snow and freezing wind. Those who lose the road during such storms often wander until exhaustion claims them.

Wolves roam the steppes in large packs, and the occasional frost troll descends from the mountains when hunger drives it southward.

The Nords also speak of stranger encounters.

Some hunters claim that shapes move across the steppe on nights when the moons are dark—figures that resemble armored warriors walking silently through the snow before vanishing into the hills. These tales are usually accompanied by the observation that the Pale contains more ancient barrows than living villages.

It is therefore unwise to travel the steppes after sunset.

---

North of Dawnstar the land falls sharply away into the jagged cliffs of the Frozen Coast, where the tundra meets the Sea of Ghosts in a maze of ice-choked inlets and black stone promontories. The coastline here is among the harshest in all of Skyrim.

Even in the warmer months great sheets of ice drift along the shore, grinding slowly against the rocks with a sound like distant thunder. In winter the entire coast becomes a frozen wilderness where only the hardiest hunters dare venture.

Horkers gather upon the ice floes in great numbers, and their thick hides provide valuable leather for the people of Dawnstar. Yet the hunters who pursue them must remain vigilant, for the ice is treacherous and the sea beneath it merciless.

It is said that the spirits of sailors lost upon the Whale Roads sometimes walk the Frozen Coast when the fog rolls in from the sea.

Whether these are truly ghosts or merely the tricks of mist and moonlight I will not pretend to say. Yet I have spoken to more than one fisherman who refuses to approach certain stretches of the coast after nightfall.

In a land already so pale and silent, even a whisper of the supernatural carries great weight.

---

Thus the Pale endures as a hold of wide skies and thin soil, where life clings stubbornly to a landscape that often seems determined to reject it.

Its people are hardy, its settlements few, and its winds unkind. Yet there is a stark beauty to the land that reveals itself only to those willing to brave its cold.

Perhaps that is why the Nords of the Pale remain so fiercely loyal to their home. For in a place where survival itself is an achievement, every winter endured becomes a victory.

And in the Pale, victories are remembered long after the victors have joined the dead beneath the tundra.

## Chapter IV: The Rift, the Valley of Red Leaves

Among the Old Holds, none appears so welcoming at first sight as The Rift.

Where the Pale lies barren and Eastmarch smolders beneath restless earth, the Rift spreads wide in a valley of forests and fertile soil. In autumn the hills blaze red and gold beneath the turning leaves, and the winds carry the scent of pine rather than the bitter chill of the northern tundra.

Many travelers from the south take this as proof that the Rift is a gentler land.

The Nords who dwell there usually laugh when they hear such things.

For while the Rift is generous to those who live upon its surface, its hills and valleys conceal a past no less old—and no less violent—than the other eastern holds.

---

At the center of the hold stands Riften, a lakeside city built along the shores of Lake Honrich. Compared with Windhelm’s towering stone walls, Riften appears almost modest, its timber halls and sloping roofs reflecting the abundant forests surrounding it.

Yet the city has long served as the southern gate of the Old Holds. Merchants from **Cyrodiil** and the distant lands beyond the Jerall Mountains frequently pass through its markets, bringing goods and stories from the wider world.

This steady flow of travelers has given Riften a reputation among the more traditional Nords as a place where foreign habits creep more easily into daily life. Yet even here the old ways endure. The mead-halls remain loud with sagas of ancient heroes, and the jarls of the Rift still trace their ancestry proudly to the early kings of Skyrim.

The city’s greatest treasure, however, is the lake beside which it stands.

---

Lake Honrich is among the largest bodies of fresh water in all of Skyrim. Its calm surface reflects the surrounding mountains like a polished mirror, and its deep waters teem with fish that sustain the towns and villages scattered along its shores. Merchant barges and fishing boats alike cross its waters daily, their sails bright against the dark blue of the lake.

Rising from the center of Honrich is a small chain of wooded islets known collectively as Skald's Retreat. These islands are reachable only by boat—or by a swim that few would willingly attempt in the cold waters of the Rift.

For generations the isles have served as a quiet sanctuary for the great bards and skalds of Skyrim. Small halls and study houses stand among the pines, their doors open to wandering singers, historians, and storytellers who seek solitude in which to compose new sagas or preserve the old ones.

The isolation of the islands is said to be deliberate. A skald who wishes to learn the true heart of a tale must listen not to the noise of cities, but to the stillness of the world itself.

Thus the waters of Honrich form both a barrier and a guardian for the retreat. When the winds fall calm and the lake grows quiet, the only sounds upon the islands are the creak of wooden halls, the scratching of quills upon parchment, and the distant call of birds circling above the water.

Some skalds claim that on such nights the lake itself begins to whisper.

They say that if one listens carefully enough, the echoes of ancient heroes drift across the dark water like half-forgotten songs.

Whether these voices belong to the past or merely to the imagination of poets is a matter upon which the scholars of the retreat have argued for centuries—and will likely continue to argue for centuries more.

---

South of the lake rises the dark mass of **Mount Fjellhorst**, a lonely peak whose steep slopes dominate the southern horizon. Near its summit stand the ruins of **Forelhost**, an ancient fortress whose history remains tangled in conflicting accounts.

Some claim the stronghold was once the refuge of a powerful dragon cult during the final years before the dragons’ fall from power. Others insist it served as a last bastion for rebels who defied the early kings of Skyrim.

Whatever the truth, the ruin has lent its name to the mountain itself, and few travelers willingly approach its shattered gates. The Nords of the Rift say the wind there carries strange whispers down the slopes at night.

Such places are rarely left alone by the past.

---

In the northern hills stands **Fallowstone Hall**, one of the great longhouses raised during the earliest centuries of Nord settlement. Tradition holds that it was built by warriors who had once fought beside the earliest champions of **The Companions**, though the exact lineage of these heroes has long since been lost to time.

Unlike many ancient halls, Fallowstone remains occupied, its hearth fires still burning after countless generations. The warriors who dwell there pride themselves on maintaining the traditions of their forebears: strength in battle, loyalty to kin, and a fierce devotion to the honor of Skyrim.

Visitors to the hall often remark that its atmosphere feels older than the surrounding villages, as though the spirits of those first warriors never entirely departed.

If that is so, they appear content to linger beside the hearth.

---

Scattered throughout the forests of the Rift are numerous ancient barrows whose origins stretch back to the earliest days of Nord rule. Among the most notable is **Angarvunde**, whose stone halls wind deep beneath the earth like the roots of an ancient tree.

The carvings upon its walls depict warriors bearing curved blades and horned helms, marching beneath banners now lost to memory. Though the runes remain partly legible, they speak mostly of battles and oaths whose meanings have faded with the centuries.

Another ruin, perched high upon a cliff overlooking the valley, is **Lost Tongue Overlook**. The name derives from an old legend claiming that a warrior once climbed the mountain seeking to learn the sacred power of the Voice from the winds themselves.

When he returned to the valley below, the story claims, he could speak only a single word of that mighty language before vanishing forever into the mountains.

Such tales are common in Skyrim, where the power of the ancient **Thu'um** remains a subject of endless fascination.

---

Among the many rivers that flow through the Rift, none carries a darker reputation than the Widow's River. Its waters wind through a narrow valley east of Lake Honrich, passing by the small settlement of Kjallgrad.

The people of this village tell a somber tale concerning the river’s name.

According to their tradition, a Nord king once rode south from Windhelm with his warband to suppress a rebellion in the Rift. During the campaign his wife remained behind in the valley, awaiting his return beside the river that flowed past their hall.

When news arrived that the king had fallen in battle, the queen is said to have walked alone to the water’s edge and vanished into its depths.

Since that night, villagers claim that a pale figure sometimes appears along the riverbank beneath the moon, watching the road as though still waiting for someone who will never return.

Whether this is truly the spirit of a grieving queen or merely the imagination of lonely shepherds, I cannot say. Yet the people of Kjallgrad speak of the matter with such quiet certainty that one hesitates to dismiss it entirely.

---

Not all ancient works of the Rift belong to the Nords.

Hidden among the mountains to the west lie the ruins of **Bthalft**, a city once built by the Dwemer, the mysterious Deep Elves whose disappearance remains one of the great riddles of Tamriel. What remains visible above ground are little more than scattered stone structures and peculiar towers of metal and carved rock.

Yet local hunters claim that deep passages descend beneath the earth below those ruins, leading into halls filled with strange machines and metal guardians that still wander the corridors of their vanished masters.

Few Nords willingly explore such places.

They prefer ruins whose builders at least possessed the courtesy to remain dead.

---

Thus the Rift endures as a land of quiet beauty layered atop ancient stories.

Its forests shelter the halls of warriors and the tombs of forgotten kings.  
Its lakes reflect mountains crowned with ruined fortresses.  
And its rivers carry whispers of grief that have endured for generations.

To the casual traveler it may seem the most peaceful of the Old Holds.

But like all of Skyrim’s eastern lands, the Rift keeps its past close at hand—and it seldom forgets those who once walked its valleys.

## Chapter V: Winterhold, the City that Speaks with the Sea

Of all the Old Holds, none inspires such mixed admiration and unease as Winterhold.

Where Windhelm boasts of kings and the Rift of fertile valleys, Winterhold claims stranger honors. Its cliffs stand high above the Sea of Ghosts, battered endlessly by wind and wave, and its towers rise like dark spears against the northern sky. The city has long served as both fortress and beacon, a place where warriors, sailors, scholars, and mystics have gathered beneath the same storm-lashed roofs.

To the Nords of the Old Holds, Winterhold is both a source of pride and a matter of quiet suspicion.

For though it is undeniably ancient, its greatest claim to fame lies not in the swords of its warriors, but in the magic of its scholars.

---

Dominating the northern cliffs of the city stands the famed College of Winterhold, an institution whose origins are attributed—by legend if not by certainty—to the archmage Shalidor.

The stories concerning its creation vary greatly depending upon the teller. Some say Shalidor raised the college stone by stone through sheer mastery of the arcane arts. Others insist that he summoned spirits of earth and wind to build it for him in a single night.

The most popular tale among the skalds, however, claims that Shalidor simply spoke the college into existence.

According to this legend, the archmage stood upon the cliffs of Winterhold and uttered a Word of Power so profound that the very stones of the mountain rose in answer, shaping themselves into the towers and halls that stand there today.

Scholars may debate the truth of this account, yet even the most skeptical observer must admit that the college possesses an atmosphere unlike any other place in Skyrim. Strange lights sometimes flicker in its upper towers, and travelers claim that its halls contain libraries older than many kingdoms.

The Nords tolerate the presence of the college largely because it has stood there for generations, and because its mages rarely interfere in the affairs of jarls.

Still, it is not uncommon to hear a fisherman mutter that too much magic invites trouble.

---

North of the city, beyond the broken cliffs and restless waves, lies the lonely island known as Ysgramor's Rest.

The small isle rises stark and grey from the waters of the Sea of Ghosts, its rocky slopes crowned by an ancient tomb whose age is matched by few structures in all of Skyrim. Tradition holds that this is the final resting place of Ysgramor himself, the great war-leader who brought the Nords from Atmora and founded the first kingdoms of men in the north.

Whether the tomb truly holds the bones of Ysgramor remains a matter of quiet debate among scholars.

The Nords themselves rarely question it.

Pilgrims from across the Old Holds sometimes attempt the crossing to the island, particularly during the summer months when the sea grows marginally less hostile. The journey is perilous even then, for the waters between the mainland and the island are treacherous and the winds unpredictable.

Yet those who reach the island speak of a powerful stillness surrounding the ancient barrow.

Some claim that the spirits of the Five Hundred Companions themselves stand watch over the tomb.

Such stories are difficult to confirm, for few visitors linger long in that lonely place.

---

The coastline surrounding Winterhold is among the most dangerous in all of Skyrim.

Glaciers descend from the mountains like frozen rivers, grinding slowly toward the sea over the course of centuries. Vast shelves of ice occasionally break free from these frozen masses, crashing into the waters below with a force capable of shattering even the sturdiest ship.

Many sailors have lost their lives along this glacial coast.

The Nords who fish these waters are accustomed to the sight of broken ice drifting across the horizon like wandering islands. They navigate carefully between these frozen obstacles, guided by knowledge passed down through generations.

Even so, the sea claims its share of victims each year.

It is said that when fog settles thick upon the water, the cries of drowning sailors sometimes echo across the waves long after their ships have vanished.

---

To the west of Winterhold the land stretches toward Dawnstar, yet the journey between these two settlements is among the harshest in the province. The road passes first through the bleak plains known as the Stornorlig Tundra, where the wind blows almost without pause across miles of barren ground.

Little grows there beyond tough grasses and scattered shrubs, and travelers often remark that the sky seems impossibly wide above the empty land.

Blizzards sweep across the tundra with little warning. A traveler who strays from the road during such storms may wander for hours without finding shelter.

Wolves and ice wraiths haunt the open plains, and more than one caravan has vanished entirely while attempting the crossing in winter.

---

Beyond the tundra the road descends into the narrow waterways known as the Hvitavfal Fjords, where the mountains plunge sharply into the sea. Here the land becomes a maze of steep cliffs, icy inlets, and winding paths carved into the rock.

The fjords are beautiful in a stark and dangerous way. Great walls of ice gleam blue beneath the pale sun, and the sea reflects the towering cliffs like polished glass when the wind falls calm.

Yet the same cliffs that create such beauty also conceal many hazards.

Avalanches sometimes thunder down from the heights without warning, burying roads beneath tons of snow and shattered ice. Sudden storms can trap travelers between the mountains and the sea with little chance of escape.

Those who make the journey between Winterhold and Dawnstar often speak of it with the same tone one might use when describing a difficult battle that was narrowly survived.

---

Thus Winterhold stands as the most northern and perhaps the most mysterious of the Old Holds.

Its city watches the sea from wind-carved cliffs.
Its mages pursue knowledge that many Nords view with cautious suspicion.
Its coast devours ships and sailors alike.
And its frozen roads test the endurance of all who travel them.

Yet for all its hardships, Winterhold endures.

Perhaps this is fitting. For a land born of storm and ice could scarcely be expected to produce anything less than a people equally unyielding.

And in the shadow of the ancient college, where the winds howl across the cliffs and the sea murmurs endlessly below, one cannot help but feel that the past—whether of kings, mages, or heroes—has not yet finished speaking.

## Chapter VI: On the Future of the Old Holds

In writing of the Old Holds, of Eastmarch, the Pale, the Rift, and Winterhold, one becomes keenly aware that these lands carry the past as heavily as a warrior bears his shield.

Every road passes near an ancient barrow.
Every river seems to remember a battle.
Every jarl claims descent from heroes whose deeds have grown larger with every telling.

It is tempting for a scholar to dismiss such claims as the natural exaggeration of a proud people. Yet to do so would be to misunderstand the Nords entirely.

For the Nords do not merely remember their past. They live beside it.

The barrows of their ancestors stand in plain view of their fields. The songs of their forebears are shouted over every mead-hall hearth. Even the land itself bears the marks of those ancient days, from the weathered stones of Windhelm to the silent island of Ysgramor's Rest rising from the Sea of Ghosts.

Yet memory is both a blessing and a burden.

For while the Old Holds preserve the traditions that shaped Skyrim, they also cling to rivalries whose origins few now recall. Jarls quarrel over lands that have changed hands a dozen times since the days of Ysgramor, and ancient grievances are carried forward as faithfully as any heirloom blade.

It is not my place to say whether this stubborn devotion to the past strengthens Skyrim or weakens it. I merely observe that the Old Holds stand much as they always have: proud, ancient, and wary of change. Perhaps this will always be so.

The winds that sweep across the Stornorlig Tundra care little for the quarrels of jarls, and the waves beneath Winterhold have eroded stone far older than any kingdom of men.

Yet the Nords endure where many others have faded.

If the sagas are to be believed, this is because they remember who they are.

And if the Old Holds remember anything at all, it is that Skyrim was born in storm and struggle, and that such storms rarely end forever.
