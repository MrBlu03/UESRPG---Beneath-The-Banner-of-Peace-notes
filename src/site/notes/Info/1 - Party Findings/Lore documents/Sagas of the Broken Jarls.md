# Sagas of the Broken Jarls

_Collected and translated by Thjorik of Shor's Stone Skald-Elder of the Rift
Written in the 67th year of the Second Era_

## Preface of the Collector

The songs gathered in this book are older than the halls in which they are sung.

They are fragments of a time when the lands of the Rift were not yet quiet valleys of farmers and traders, but a patchwork of rival jarldoms scattered between forest and mountain. In those elder centuries many jarls rose to power with great promise, only to fall in misfortune, treachery, or folly.

Such rulers the skalds call the Broken Jarls.

The word does not mean cowardice, nor does it mean weakness. A Broken Jarl is one whose story ends in ruin, a crown lost, a hall burned, a name remembered only in warning.

In gathering these sagas I have listened to wandering skalds, hunters of the foothills, and elders who remember songs sung by their grandfathers. Much has been lost, and what remains is tangled with embellishment and pride.

Still, the bones of truth lie beneath the verses, and it is better that these bones be preserved than allowed to vanish entirely.

Thus I set them down here.

---

## First Lay

**The Jarl Who Crowned Himself Twice**

In the years when the valley roads were still young, there ruled in the southern Rift a jarl named Rorik the Bold.

He was a warrior of great strength and greater ambitions. His hall stood upon a ridge above the forests, and from its gates he declared that all the valleys below belong to him by right of conquest.

Rorik wore two crowns.

The first was the iron circlet of his father, a chieftain's crown forged in the elder days. The second he had made himself from silver taken in raids upon caravans from Cyrodiil.

When the skalds asked why he wore both, Rorik answered:
"Because one crown is not enough for the jarl who will rule all the Rift."

But the gods are wary of men who crown themselves too highly.

Rorik gathered warriors and marched to claim the eastern valleys. His victories were swift at first, and many smaller chiefs bent the knee before him.

Yet his triumph bred envy. Three rival jarls met in secret beneath the pines and swore to break the upstart king. They fell upon Rorik's warband in the night as it camped beside a narrow river.

By morning the silver crown lay buried in mud, and Rorik lay beside it.

The iron crown was carried back to his hall. The silver one was never found again.

Thus the skalds say that no man should wear two crowns unless he wishes to lose them both.

## Second Lay

**The Burning of Hroldvar's Hall**

There was once a jarl named Hroldvar who ruled the western woods of the Rift.

His hall stood deep among the pines, where the wind seldom reached and the forest paths twisted like snakes. Hroldvar was a clever ruler, but he trusted too greatly in walls and warriors.

One winter he seized the lands of a neighboring clan, claiming their chieftain had insulted him at a feast.

The insult may have been real, or it may have been imagined. Skalds disagree.

What all agree upon is the chieftain's sons did not forgive the insult.

For three years they said nothing.

Then, on the coldest night of the fourth winter, they returned.

The brothers crept through the forest with torches wrapped in oilcloth to hide their flames. While the warriors of Hroldvar slept, they set fire to the wooden roofs of the hall.

The blaze spread quickly through the dry timbers.

By the time Hroldvar awoke, the roof had already begun to fall.

The jarl escaped the flames, but his hall was lost, and the clans who once followed him drifted away to serve other lords.

Hroldvar lived the rest of his days in a smaller house beside a river.

When skalds asked what he had learned, he answered:
"A hall is strong, but pride burns hotter."

## Third Lay

**Third lay, as referenced by E. in the margins of On the Old Holds**

_Concerning Hadring the Unlucky and the Widow of the Eastern March_

Hear now the third lay, as sung in the old halls when the fire burns low and the mead runs thin. This is the tale of Jarl Hadring, called the Unlucky by those who lived after him, though none dared speak the name while he yet sat upon the high seat.

In those days the eastern valleys of the Rift were not yet quiet lands of farmers and traders, but wild country where jarls rose and fell as quickly as storms on the mountain passes. It was a time when men measured their borders not by stone markers but by how far their warbands could ride before nightfall.

Hadring was a strong jarl, broad of shoulder and fierce in battle. His hall stood beside the river that now bears the name Widow's River, though in those days it was called simply the Whiteflow. His warriors were many, and his enemies more so, for Hadring possessed the temper of a winter gale and the patience of a hungry wolf.

Yet he was not alone upon his high seat.

His wife, Lady Yrsa of the northern clans, was said to be wise than her husband in all matters save the sword. The skalds remember her as sharp of tongue and steady of mind, a woman who spoke little but saw much. Some say that Hadring ruled the valley with his axe, while Yrsa ruled it with her counsel.

For many winter theirs hall prospered.

But fortune is a fickle companion, and the Norns seldom grant a jarl too many victories.

It was in the twelfth year of Hadring's rule that war came riding down from the mountain passes. A rival lord, whose name the skalds no longer sing, for he died without glory, gathered warriors from the hills and swore to take the valley for his own.

Hadring laughed when he heard of it.

"Let him come," the jarl said, raising his drinking horn before the gathered hall. "The crows of the Rift have grown lean this winter."

And so the warband rode out beneath his banner.

They marched south along the river road, their shield bright in the morning sun. Lady Yrsa watched them depart from the steps of the hall, her cloak drawn tight against the wind.

Some say she spoke a warning then, though no two tellings agree on the words.

What all agree upon is that Hadring did not heed them.

Three days later the valley heard the distant thunder of battle.

The fight was long and bitter in the narrow pass where the mountains close like the jaws of a wolf. Spears broke, shields split, and the snow ran red beneath the boots of dying men.

When the battle ended, the rival lord lay dead upon the field. So too did Jarl Hadring.

A messenger carried the news back along the frozen road to the hall beside the river. The skalds say that Lady Yrsa listened in silence while the warrior spoke, her face still as carved stone.

When the tale was done she dismissed the hall and walked alone to the riverbank.

The moon had risen by then, and the waters of the Whiteflow shone pale beneath its light.

There the widow of Hadring stood for a long while, watching the dark current slip past the stones. None know what thoughts passed through her mind, for no servant dared approach her.

At last she laid aside her cloak and stepped into the water.

The river took her before dawn.

When the warriors of the hall found the empty bank at morning light, they raised a cairn beside the river and spoke no word of the matter for many years.

Yet travelers began to whisper that on moonlit nights a pale figure could sometimes be seen standing beside the water, gazing down the southern road as though waiting for a warband that would never return.

And so the Whiteflow was given a new name by those who passed that way.

They called it the Widow's River, and the valley remembered.

This ends the third lay of the Broken Jarls, a warning sung in quiet halls: that even the strongest jarl may fall to fate, and that the grief of those left behind may linger longer than any crown.

## Fourth Lay

**The Three Stone Brothers**

Among the weathered tales of the Rift there is frequent mention of three standing stones in the foothills west of the trading roads. Most travelers know them only as strange pillars of rock that rise from the earth like the ribs of some buried giant.

In the oldest songs they are named Three Stone Brothers, though the verses disagree on who these brothers truly were.

One sage claims they were once jarls of the mountain clans, sons of a single father who ruled the high valleys in the elder days of Skyrim. Their names are given in broken form in several fragments, Skorvald, Hjorn, and Valdyr, though none of these names appear in the later genealogies of the Rift's jarls.

The tale says the brothers once swore a sacred oath beneath the open sky, binding themselves to stand against a treacherous king who had broken faith with their people. The name of that king is curiously absent from the oldest verses, as though later skalds feared to speak it.

When war came, the brothers led their warriors down from the mountains to confront this oath-breaker. Yet the battle that followed is described only in riddling phrases.

One fragment reads:

> "The wind saw their oath,
> And the mountain heard their fall.
> Where three brothers stood as men,
> Three stones remain for all."

According to later storytellers, the brothers died together upon a ridge above the river valley. Some say Kyne herself turned them to stone so their oath would stand forever. Others claim the stones mark the place where their bodies were burned upon a single pyre.

Whatever the truth, the stones have stood since long before the present holds were founded.

The local hunters of the eastern forests still use them as a landmark. They say the pillars rise from a narrow ridge overlooking the bend of an old river, a place where the mountains begin to swallow the lowlands.

Few linger there after nightfall. The stones are said to cast long shadows even beneath the moon, and many claim the wind around them carries the sound of distant war-horns.

## Fifth Lay

**The Jarl Who Spoke to the Mountain**

The last of the Broken Jarls remembered by the skalds is Vargeir the Listener.

Vargeir ruled a small valley near the eastern cliffs. Unlike many jarls he was not know for his strength in battle, but for the strange habit of wandering alone into the mountains to "listen to the voices of the stone."

Some believed him touched by the gods.

Others believed him mad.

One autumn he returned from the peaks and declared that the mountain itself had warned him of a great war soon to fall upon the Rift.

He ordered his people to abandon their fields and follow him north to safer lands.

The clans refused.

They said the mountain had spoken only to his imagination.

Vargeir left alone with a handful of loyal warriors.

Years passed, and the jarl's hall slowly emptied as his followers joined other clans. 

What became of Vargeir none can say. Some claim he vanished into the mountains forever. Others insist he found another valley and ruled there in peace.

Yet the skalds end his story with a final verse:

> "The jarl who hears the mountain's breath
> Must choose between crown and stone.
> For a king who walks with the wind
> Will never rule long at home."

---

## Closing Words of the Skald

These are the tales that remain of the Broken Jarls.

Their halls have fallen.
Their crowns are dust.
Their lands belong to other men.

Yet their stories endure, carried in song from hearth to hearth across the valley of the Rift.

For the Nords remember that a crown is never secure, and that the difference between a great king and a Broken Jarl may be no more than a single winter's fortune.

So the sagas are sung, not to mock the fallen, but to remind those who rule today that every throne stands upon uncertain ground.

## Marginalia

**Beside the Fourth Lay**

_"There is mention in another damaged chronicle of three mountain jarls who rose against a treacherous ruler in the eastern valleys. See the fragment attributed to Brother Vethran of Riften describing the so-called King Without Honor. The account claims the jarls united to overthrow an oath-breaker who had deceived them all.
It is tempting to wonder whether these jarls are the same three remembered here as Skorvald, Hjorn, and Valdyr. If so, the 'treacherous king whose name is not spoken' in this lay may be none other than that same oath-breaker whose memory the skalds tried to erase.
The silence of the verses may therefore be deliberate._
- Annotation signed T.E., believed to be the historial Torald Erennius
