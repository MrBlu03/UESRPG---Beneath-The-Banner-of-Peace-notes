## Chapter 32 – Hammer on Anvil

**Loredas, 13th of Evening Star, 2E 600**

The morning rose still smelling of guilt.

By the time the Fangs awoke, it was already near noon — the tomb holding them in its sleep as though unwilling to release what they had done, or what had been done to them. No one had died in that ruin. And yet no one who had walked its halls last night would call it a clean victory.

Periwyn rose first, or at least rose worst, still carrying her guilt like a stone in her cloak. She gathered her things quickly and took a seat near the unconscious priest — near, but not beside — as though proximity itself were a form of penance.

Durov was already inside, eating rations and working over his newly acquired recipe for Sweet Pork, testing the substitutions the tomb's meager larders demanded. When Periwyn arrived, he looked her way at once — and did not let her leave his gaze.

Before she had even sat down, he told her, without ceremony, that he hoped this was a one-time thing.

By the time Onasha arrived, the sweet pork was done. Durov handed her a full portion, called the Vestige over for his, kept one for himself — and set down a measly half-portion in front of Periwyn, whose crime was apparently still under review. As they had no pork among their stores, Durov had substituted wolf meat: the result was heartier, gamier than the original, but it held, unmistakably, the essence of sweetness.

Orgnar's recipe, it seemed, could survive even the end of the inn that birthed it.

After they had eaten, Onasha made a proposal: return to the Dwemer ruin and pillage it before other brigands or miscreants got there first. She had her own loot to collect, and wished to examine the animunculi the Vestige had supposedly fought. Periwyn was eager to join — for what better ground for another lesson with the Tome than the site of such death? — and Durov came too, keen to melt down his own spoils and dig through whatever remained of the scrap machines.

He left last.

So as not to leave Periwyn alone with the priest.

---

The ruin was still in the same state they had left it: full of dried blood and corpses, the halls silent in a way no hall should be. Durov and Onasha made their way through the various rooms in search of scrap and useful reagents, gathering any worthwhile weapons and armor to melt down, and carrying them to the central smithing chamber.

Durov, for all his skill at the forge, did not know his way around machinery such as this. All he could piece together on his own was that the room was lined with smelters of some kind, their heating elements fed from below by numerous pipes, controlled by valves.

Onasha offered more insight.

Studying the machines — the connections, the openings, the shoots — she managed to piece together the various controls. At the bottom, a valve regulated the influx of lava from the pipes: this was what heated the compartment in the middle, which was opened by a lever near the top right, where whatever needed melting would be placed. In the top left sat another valve, with a subtler purpose. When the compartments were sealed, pressure would build inside the smelter. If it ran too high — beyond what the machine could contain — it would end in explosion, and the destruction of the whole apparatus.

Durov made a mental note of this, in the manner of a man who had recently pulled one lever too many.

While the smelter heated, the two went hunting for animunculi. This led them all across the ruin, and eventually to what looked like a storeroom the Cult had repurposed into sleeping quarters. Several piles of Dwemer metal lay strewn across the floor. It took some prodding and some careful tinkering, but between them they salvaged what remained: soul gems, a measure of Dwemer oil, and plenty of scrap for Durov to render down into usable material.

They both returned just in time to let some pressure out of the smelter — and went to work.

Onasha, with no experience melting down metals, elected to keep her distance, standing back near one of the doors where she could simply watch Durov work without the oppression of the smelter's heat. The man stripped off his armor, leaving only breeches and a shirt to better bear the blaze.

From her angle, the view was mostly obstructed. But she caught glimpses of the man he was beneath the steel.

Broad-jawed. Well-kept hair. A beard trimmed with care, no grey in it anywhere.

It was a stark contrast to the gruff exterior his armor presented — though, in hindsight, it should not have surprised her. His armor, too, was always impeccably maintained.

---

Elsewhere in the ruin, Periwyn's list of reckless, impulsive, and downright moronic ideas grew by one.

This lesson, Mortgren's Tome had given her, was to sense the last emotions of the dead.

And here, laid out in a chamber still reeking of violence, lay a Xivilai.

She asked herself, reasonably enough: what could possibly go wrong? It was not as though she would turn magic intended to read the emotions of a restless mortal soul upon an immortal Daedra whose vestige had already fled back to Oblivion.

She would, of course.

The moment her magic coursed between herself and the body, everything changed.

Periwyn found herself standing in an endless void — filled with nothing but darkness... and six doors. No numbers upon them, no colors, not even cracks. Six identical doors, in a blackness without floor or sky.

She opened the first, and found a serene countryside stretching to the horizon: majestic cities seemingly made of glass, ornate towers rising among dense forests, beautiful mountains, floating stone pillars scattered across the landscape like forgotten monuments.

That was the *look* of the place.

The *feeling* was singular, and cruel.

Regret.

And the regret took form for her — as the Priest of Stendarr, standing in the pastoral distance, with burning coals where his eyes should have been.

Sensing, with uncommon clarity, that this place was not real, Periwyn turned and ran back through the door, unwilling to feel any more intense regret that day.

The door she fled through next was kinder.

She emerged into a vast land of floating rocks, coral reefs, and luminescent water, beneath skies like drifting oceans of crimson effluvium. Curiosity got the better of her again — this time, for the better. She bent down and reached into the water: solid enough to walk upon, yet able to be scooped up and drunk all the same. She drank, and felt as though every ounce of her mental exhaustion had been swept away in a single breath.

As a small experiment, she filled her empty flask with the stuff, and returned to the void.

Alas — the third door would have her wishing she had never left the first.

---

Dust. Smoke. Ash.

The air was thick with all three, and visibility so poor that Periwyn could barely see the magic glowing in her own hands. Then, out of the haze, three figures emerged.

Vampires.

And Periwyn was filled with a single emotion, so strong she could not have fought it if she had tried. Rage. Irresistible. Total. Suffocating, like the air itself.

She drew her sword and coaxed one of the creatures away from the others, to take it on alone.

And take it on she did — rather quickly. Too quickly, perhaps. A single blow to its chest, and the vampire turned to ash, scattering on the wind.

Far off, across this ashen country, metal was being shaped. A hammer striking an anvil. Once. Loud. All-consuming.

Periwyn turned her rage upon the remaining two.

And for a moment — one singular, brief moment — she was not looking at monsters at all. She was looking at a woman and a young boy.

But that did not matter. Not when vengeance was filling her head, crowding out everything else.

With a single, terrible ray of light, she turned them both to ash, and watched them blow away in the wind just the same.

And again, hammer on anvil. Twice.

When a moment of clarity finally found her, she turned back toward the door.

But something did not want her to leave. Not just yet.

Standing before it was an orc — horns rounding his head, skin like the ash upon the air — and he stood taller than the door itself, blocking it entirely.

And he was cross.

This was his home, you see. His garden. His front yard, if you will. And Periwyn was standing in it, hurting his guests. And he had not invited her. Not this time, at least.

Periwyn — because of course she did — dared to speak out against the lord of the house, telling him to go away and let her leave.

It was then that the cogs in her head finally clicked into place.

This rage. This overwhelming, drowning urge for vengeance. It was not hers alone. It was *him*. This place where every breath hurt — this ashpit—

*The* Ashpit.

And standing before her, barring the door, was its master.

Malacath. The Daedric Prince of Vengeance.

She had heard him before — in her nightmares, coaxing her onward, urging her deeper into her crusade, into the bloody oath she had sworn. And he was there to do the same once more.

Knowing how grief and anger filled her mind, he offered her a boon.

A hammer. Of his own making. A tool for her vengeance.

Periwyn declined.

Quite angrily, it must be said.

This was *her* crusade. Hers alone. Of course she still wished to destroy every last bloodsucker — but she would do it on her own merits, with her own strength. No god's help. No borrowed fury. Just her, and her alone. And He was standing in her way.

In her rage, Periwyn flung her blinding light directly into the Prince's face.

He simply scoffed — and picked her up like a wet rag, swearing up and down that she would pay for her offense.

It was then that Periwyn took her sword and plunged it into His chest.

The sword had belonged to her mother, Sapphirelle.

It was the last physical possession of hers that Periwyn still had. A beautiful longsword of silvered steel, its blade straight and double-edged, with a fuller running nearly its whole length — etched with the complete lunar cycle of Masser and Secunda: twenty-four tablets wreathed in stars, and the faint tracery of constellations. The crossguard looked less forged than grown, its tendrils of silvered steel sweeping from the blade's shoulders like roots finding soil — some curling low along the blade to guard the hand, others reaching briefly outward before tapering to fine points. And at its heart, where all the roots converged, sat a small silvered crescent moon, polished bright against the darkened steel, as though light had been caught among branches.

Her mother had named it *Secunda's Bloom*.

But when it met the hide of the Lord of Ash and Bone — it simply shattered.

Periwyn's rage doubled.

She took the last fragment still attached to the hilt and drove it into Malacath's arm, piercing His hide. This offended the God of Curses, who seized her tightly by the neck and slammed her against the ground behind Him. As He reached for His mighty maul, Periwyn gathered her strength just in time to shimmer away — and slip back through the door.

And she was in the Dwemer ruin again.

As if no time had passed at all. As if none of it had been real.

Or so she thought.

She looked into her flask — and saw the same luminescent water she had scooped up in that other world, still glowing faintly, like a captured piece of a kinder place.

Then it dawned on her.

Her sword.

She drew it from its sheath, and there it was.

Shards.

The sword she had carried for eighteen years. Broken.

She sat with it for a moment. Then, quietly, she gathered the pieces and slid them back into the sheath — and returned to the main chamber to finish her final lesson.

Back in the chamber, the blood had dried solid upon the stone. Periwyn looked around and found one of the drained corpses — one of the guards whose life had been siphoned away. She sat beside it for a time. Then she took its hand, and reached for its soul.

What she felt was nothing but disturbing.

The air in her lungs was forced out at once — pulled from her like rope from a well. Her thoughts, her blood, her very life essence felt as though they were being drawn out of her, drained by a hunger so vast it had never once been sated. This time, the mercy of death did not come — not until her concentration broke and she fell backward, still barely able to breathe.

Whatever had done this to the guard was something she had never felt, never seen, never imagined. Such hunger. Such dread.

It was a force no living thing could bear.

---

By the time Durov and Onasha were done, Periwyn was already long gone.

Onasha returned straight to the Oath-Breaker's Tomb.

Durov, however, made a stop of his own — to the library, in search of knowledge about what precisely he had seen beneath that mountain.

Two books caught his eye at once: *Olaf and the Dragon*, and *The Alduin/Akatosh Dichotomy*. He read them thoroughly at a library table, marking passages and trying to piece together what they told him.

From *Olaf and the Dragon*, he learned the old tale of Olaf One-Eye — the Jarl who, so the bards insisted, had battled the dragon Numinex and kept the great wyrm caged in Dragonsreach for generations, a trophy and a prisoner both. Whether the tale was true or embellished, the lesson it carried was clear enough: dragons could be beaten, bound, even caged — but never truly tamed, and never truly killed.

From *The Alduin/Akatosh Dichotomy*, he took away something stranger. That the Nordic people had once worshipped Alduin — the World-Eater — as their chief god, and that Akatosh, the dragon-god of the Empire, was a different understanding of the same divine shape, split apart by priests and scholars over centuries. That dragons were not merely beasts but the children — or the fragments — of something divine. And that where a dragon walked, it did so because something older had made the world a place where dragons *could* walk.

Durov closed both books with the same unanswered question burning in him: what was one doing, sealed beneath a mountain, still breathing, still watching?

---

He then stopped by Orgnar's, for a good meal and better company.

The two of them spoke at length about Durov's substitution of wolf meat in the recipe — Orgnar approving of the pragmatism, though mourning, with a craftsman's precision, the loss of the pork fat that carried the sweetness through. They argued the merits of different cures, the proper sear, whether juniper belonged in the glaze or in the pan beneath it. Orgnar shared his trick for keeping the glaze from burning: a whisper of snow-cold water flicked over the meat at just the right moment, hissing the sugar down before it could catch.

Durov helped Orgnar around the inn while they talked — hauling casks, mending a hinge, restacking wood by the hearth. When he finished, Orgnar reached for his coin purse, and Durov waved him off.

He would not take coin for it.

Not today. Not from this man.

Orgnar seemed to understand exactly what the refusal meant, and accepted it with a nod that carried more warmth than any thanks he could have spoken. Some debts, between friends, were not meant to be settled — only remembered.

---

Back at the tomb, Periwyn told Onasha everything — the whole vision, the whole encounter — as the two of them cared for the horses, brushing them down and speaking in the low, unhurried way that stable work demands.

Periwyn confessed her deepest fear.

That her violent past, and the crusade it had become, drove away everyone who ever got close. She spoke of the alchemist — the one from the drawing Onasha had seen in her journal, days before, seated beside her at a campfire beneath scratched-in stars. The one who had left, not in anger, but because Periwyn's road had become too heavy for two.

Onasha reassured her, simply and without ceremony.

She was unafraid of Periwyn's demons.

For who in this tomb did not have plenty?

By the time Durov returned, Periwyn had already heard an earful from the Vestige about her encounter with Malacath. Not much of it was memorable — mostly the poor Dunmer groaning and sighing at the thought of this Breton continuing to neither think ahead nor know when to quit. He swore, with the weary certainty of a prophecy, that her impulsivity would get her killed one day.

Durov found her sitting by her horse, Ashen, the pieces of her mother's sword laid out before her on a cloth.

He looked them over, testing whether it might be repaired — but the Vestige chimed in before he could finish.

Only one smith alive had a chance at restoring it, he said: Hroldan Fire-Eye. The legendary blacksmith who had forged the Vestige's own weapons. His forge lay somewhere in the mountains north of Windhelm — and with the passes to Eastmarch blocked by snowfall, the Fangs had a long while to wait before they could make that trek.

So the shards would wait too.

Periwyn gathered them up, wrapped them carefully, and stored them away — not as a sword any longer, but as something else. A promise that had survived its own breaking.

And somewhere, far above the world of men, in a garden of ash, a hammer fell upon an anvil.

Once.

Twice.

Waiting.
