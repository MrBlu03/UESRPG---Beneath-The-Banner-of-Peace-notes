## Chapter 29 – The Sun Beneath the Mountain

**Turdas, 11th of Evening Star, 2E 600 (deep night)**

The ruin slept, but not all within it did.

Past midnight, when even the draugr seemed to forget their centuries, Onasha and Periwyn found they had no wish for sleep. Their conversation by the fire had grown too honest to abandon, and so they took it with them, wrapping cloaks about their shoulders and slipping out beneath the broken arch of the tomb's threshold.

The world beyond was still.

Snow lay undisturbed in every direction, pale beneath a sky neither wholly dark nor wholly lit. Above the pines, the aurora burned — not the green of harvest tales, but a strange yellow-green, like old brass held against a candleflame. It swayed without wind, and its light made no shadows.

They walked without purpose until purpose found them: a small frozen pond, perhaps fifty paces from the ruin, its surface dark and flawless as polished obsidian. They sat upon a fallen stone at its edge.

For a while, neither spoke.

It was Onasha who broke the silence at last. She had been watching Periwyn's profile against the aurora, and something in her could not reconcile it.

_"How do you keep finding the energy?"_ she asked. _"To be angry as you are. In every fight. Every day. It does not seem to fade."_

Periwyn did not answer immediately. Her breath rose in white ribbons and dissolved.

_"It is not anger alone,"_ she said finally. _"It is a promise. I made it — to myself, and to him. To my brother."_ Her voice was steady, but low, the way one speaks of things long carried. _"As long as I can keep that promise, I can keep living. That is what it is for."_

She turned then, and there was something raw and unguarded in her face that Onasha had rarely seen.

_"I know what it costs. I know it is not healthy — I am not blind to it."_ A faint, bitter smile. _"So do not be like me, Onasha. Do not draw upon those parts of yourself. Find another fire. Promise me that much, at least."_

The aurora shifted above them, slow as breathing.

Onasha asked what her brother had been like.

And Periwyn — who so often guarded her past behind a wall of duty and steel — opened it willingly.

_"A prodigy,"_ she said, and there was pride in it, and grief in equal measure. _"Far more gifted than I ever was. The arcane came to him like water finding a slope. I was the one always buried in books while he simply... understood."_ She laughed softly, without humour. _"He would have found the thing in Greenwall's cellar in an afternoon, and explained it to me by supper."_

Then, from within her cloak, she drew her journal.

It was a heavy thing — swollen with pages, ink, and charcoal, its covers worn smooth by years of hands. She set it upon her knee and turned through it slowly, and Onasha saw the life of a family unfold beneath her fingertips. Her mother, drawn with careful lines. Her father. Her mentor of the temple. And her brother — again and again, page after page, rendered at different ages. The earliest portraits were precise, loving, true to memory. But as the pages turned, something in them changed. The faces grew softer. Less certain. Imagined, where memory could no longer reach.

_"These are the ones I drew after,"_ Periwyn said quietly. _"When I could no longer remember, I drew what I believed he would have become."_

Between the pages, pressed flat and browned with age, lay a single dried flower. Delicate. Five petals the colour of dusk.

_"A periwinkle,"_ she said, when Onasha touched it gently. _"My mother loved picking them. I draw them still — for them, all of them. And sometimes I leave them with the dead. A remembrance, so that no one passes unremembered."_ She closed the book with care, as one closes a door upon sleeping children. _"It has become a reflex now. My hands do it even when my mind forgets why."_

Onasha was silent for a long moment.

Then she spoke — hesitantly at first, then with a steadiness that surprised even herself.

She spoke of a girl she had left behind, long ago, on the night she escaped slavery. Her name, she said, was Xula. She would have been barely an adult by now. And though Onasha's memory was a fortress — perfect, exhaustive, merciless in what it retained — it was Xula's face that haunted her most. Not for what she remembered.

For what she feared.

_"That she never forgave me. For leaving her there."_ Her voice frayed at the edges. _"That every day I am free is a day I abandoned her."_

Periwyn shook her head, and her hand found Onasha's shoulder.

_"Listen to me."_ Her voice was gentle, but without doubt. _"Someone like you is not easily forgotten. Whatever she feels — anger, grief, longing — she feels it because you mattered. That is not the same as condemnation. People remember those who leave. They forget no one else so thoroughly."_

The aurora unbraided and rewove itself above the pines.

And then something dawned across Onasha's face — a realisation arriving slowly, like frost forming on glass.

She had been looking at the portraits of Periwyn's family, at faces preserved line by line against oblivion, when the thought struck her: she could not have done the same.

Her memory was flawless in nearly all things — faces of strangers, debts, distances, the words of dead men. Except one.

Her parents' faces.

When she reached for them, she found only a haze — a smear of indistinct features where two people should have stood. Her own mind, she realised, had quietly walled away the one thing she had never chosen to lose.

She said nothing of it for a long while. When she finally spoke, it was only to note it, flatly, the way one reports a wound already bandaged.

The true thread of the night, though neither named it, lay elsewhere.

Each of them, in her own way, had been forced to sit across from the thing she hated most. For Periwyn, it was the vampires — the hunger that had unmade her family, her temple, her life. For Onasha, it was the Dunmer — the slavers of her people, the chains, the whips, the faces at the auction block.

And neither had found her hatred to be as simple as it once was.

Onasha admitted it first, and with visible discomfort. The Vestige, she said, had only ever helped them. Had never wronged her, nor the Fangs. And she was struggling — struggling — to hold onto fear and hatred for someone who had done nothing to earn them.

_"I can scarcely look him in the eyes,"_ she confessed, _"without my heart trying to leave my chest. And I do not know if what I feel is memory, or merely habit wearing memory's face."_

Periwyn considered this, and answered with careful honesty.

_"My own line is not so clean as it once was,"_ she said. _"Dunmer are not what vampires are. They are not evil by their nature — they are people, and people choose."_ She paused, and her gaze drifted to the black ice of the pond. _"But vampires..."_

She did not finish.

And Onasha, watching her, understood the unfinished sentence for what it was. Periwyn still spoke of vampires as she always had — but the certainty beneath the words had thinned. Somewhere between Greenwall and the mountains, something had begun to erode. Onasha could see it, even if Periwyn could not yet name it.

At length the conversation turned, almost by accident, to legacy.

Onasha asked whether Periwyn's words to the merchants of Kjallgrad — the talk of restoring her family's trading roots — had been true, or merely a banner convenient to carry.

Periwyn was quiet for a time.

_"It was not true,"_ she admitted. _"Not at first. I said it because it sounded noble, and because I needed something to say that was not vengeance."_ Then she sighed, and something shifted in her voice — the sound of a woman surprising herself. _"But it may have changed my mind. Somewhat."_

She spoke of her family as she rarely did: not as the dead, but as builders. A trade network that had spanned half the continent. Caravans and contracts. Ships and warehouses. Grain, cloth, iron — the quiet, unglamorous work of feeding and clothing people across nations, and of being remembered for it.

_"Perhaps that is part of my crusade too,"_ she said. _"Not only to avenge what was taken. But to honour what was built."_

Then she opened the journal once more — not to her own drawings, but to one she turned to with visible hesitation.

It was not by her hand. The lines were surer, the style another's entirely. It showed two figures seated at a small campfire, seen from behind, beneath a sky of loosely scratched stars. One was unmistakably Periwyn.

The other, she explained softly, was an old companion from High Rock. Someone who had once walked beside her through those early, burning years — and who had eventually left her. Not in anger, and not in betrayal. But because Periwyn's relentless crusade, and the nightmares that came with it, had become a road too heavy for two. Before she departed, her companion had told her that perhaps they would meet again — after Periwyn was finished, if finishing was ever possible.

_"I no longer know if I would wish to see her,"_ Periwyn said at last. _"Perhaps it is kinder to let her live a happy life. One I am no longer certain I deserve."_

The pond held its silence.

They sat a while longer, saying little. Then Onasha, in one of those small acts by which people say far more than they know, crouched at the pond's edge and attempted to draw a periwinkle in the snow with one finger.

The result was a tragedy.

Periwyn watched with rising amusement, then took pity. She knelt beside her, wrapped her hand around Onasha's, and guided it — stroke by stroke, petal by petal — until something resembling a flower emerged from the ruin of the first attempt.

_"There,"_ she said. _"Barely. But there."_

It was long past the deepest hour of night when Onasha's head came gently to rest upon Periwyn's shoulder, and her breathing slowed into sleep.

Periwyn did not move for some time.

At last she woke her, gently, as one wakes someone from a dream worth finishing. And together they walked back through the snow to the ruin, to what rest remained before dawn — and before their prisoner woke.

**Fredas, 12th of Evening Star, 2E 600**

Durov woke to stone.

The great ceilings and walls of the Cult's ruin stood about him in the grey half-light, unchanged since yesterday and yet somehow unfamiliar, the way a room always is before one has truly learned it. He rose, broke his fast without ceremony, and set about the only discipline he trusted: mapping.

He walked the corridors at his leisure, sketching each passage and chamber into memory and margin. The sleeping quarters first, then the halls beyond. It was on one such wandering that he found it — a door he had not opened before, set low in an alcove of worked stone. It moved sluggishly when he tested it, as though reluctant, or unused.

He pushed it open.

Beyond lay a long, straight corridor, cold and silent, running to a round chamber at its end. At the centre of that chamber stood a lever of some sort, ancient and substantial, its handle worn smooth by hands long vanished.

Durov regarded it with a soldier's suspicion, and a mercenary's curiosity.

He had time to spare.

He pulled the lever.

The world lurched.

For one sickening heartbeat he felt himself falling — the entire room dropping away beneath his boots, walls and ceiling sliding upward past him. He huddled low in the centre of the chamber, braced for impact, every instinct screaming.

The impact never came.

The descent continued — but slowly. Far too slowly for any fall. Durov rose by degrees, wariness giving way to wonder, and stood at the centre of the platform as it sank through the bones of the mountain. Walls and pillars slid past, strata of ancient stone, layer upon layer of a world built and abandoned long before any living memory.

He watched for nearly five minutes.

Then his eyes beheld something impossible.

The walls opened.

There was no ceiling. No floor, at least none that belonged to any world he knew. The platform hung suspended upon four vast pillars, descending through open air — and beyond it stretched a cavern that had no right to exist.

Fungi.

They rose from the unseen floor in forests — growths larger than castles, towering in ranks to a distant horizon, glowing blue and purple and magenta, their light pooling in the low places like spilled starlight. They stretched so far that their glow defined the edge of the world. The air was warm. Damp. Alive.

This was no mine. Nothing so small, so touched, so degraded as a mine.

Far off in the gloom, he saw them: structures. Buildings — cities, almost — raised in the same severe style as the ruins above, their towers and causeways unlit and unbroken. No smoke rose from them. No lamp moved within them.

No one had been there.

Not a single sapient soul, likely, since the Dwemer themselves had vanished from the world.

Below, a cobbled road stretched out from the platform's destination into the glowing distance, patient as an invitation. Durov considered it for a long moment.

Then, with a soldier's restraint, he decided not to follow it. Not yet.

Instead he drew a scrap of paper from his satchel, scribbled his name upon it, and hung it from one of the pillars — a traveler's mark, left for whoever might come after. _Durov was here,_ it said, in the plain hand of a man who distrusted flourishes.

Then he climbed back onto the platform and pulled the lever once more.

As he rose, he leaned out over the rail and let his gaze wander across that impossible horizon one last time — and stopped.

There was something out there.

Far in the distance, a light hung in the air like a second sun, blazing gold-white above the fungal canopy, casting the land around it in a circle of false daylight.

But the sun was flickering.

And now and again its light was eclipsed — by a silhouette. Vast. Winged.

Wings.

Durov stared, telling himself it was some bird grown huge in the strange air, or some Dwemer contraption still circling after untold centuries. Doubt made him bold. He cupped his hands and called out across the cavern, a challenge and a question flung at the distant light.

It replied.

The roar that answered him was monstrous — visceral, ancient, and vast, a sound that did not merely strike the ears but shook the soul, rolling across the glowing forest like the voice of the mountain itself given breath.

Any man of sound judgment would have gone silent.

What came over Durov that day, no one truly knows.

He called out once more.

The reply came sooner.

And closer.

By the time the platform rose past the cavern's ceiling and sealed the impossible world behind its stone, Durov's mind was racing. A dragon. Harbinger of the End Times. Blotting out the Sun Under the Mountain.

What was it doing there?

How could a dragon find itself buried beneath the earth at all?

The questions would burn in his head like the fungus-light itself — long after he had climbed back into the cold and ordinary world above.

---

Outside the ruin, the morning had arrived thin and bright. Durov put the strange cavern from his face — as much as he was able — and saw to Rhymefang, grooming the great horse with the steady, wordless care that was as near as he came to prayer.

At length, Irlav and Proventus emerged from the ruins.

The trio set out to the southeast, three riders and the crunch of snow, the forest standing white and silent about them. The sun, well risen, struck the frost-laden branches and scattered the light into a thousand small fires.

Nearly an hour's ride brought them to it: a derelict camp, hunched in a clearing like something that had died where it stood.

The snow told the tale plainly enough. Two weeks, perhaps three, since any living hand had touched the place. Bodkin arrows still bristled from the nearby trees — some of them darkened with blood long since dried to rust and black. This had been no departure. This had been an attack.

Shattered glass lay thinly beneath the snow across the whole of the camp, still faintly slick with residue. Alchemical, by its smell and sheen — the debris of bombs or flasks spent in violence. Further in, a broken cart sat half-buried, its cargo long since scattered or taken.

Durov remembered his work for Stryder in Faldarstad, and the carts that carried more than their manifests confessed. He set to it without hesitation, tearing open the cart's boards in search of hidden compartments.

He found nothing but more snow.

Near the remains of the campfire, a tent lay half-collapsed beneath the drifts. Within it, one solitary note — damp, freezing, its ink feathered by frost — had survived.

Four words.

_Attacked. New camp. East._

They read it, and turned their faces east.

**Back in the Tomb of the Oath-Breaker King**

Onasha and Periwyn were woken by the Argonian assassin, whose tact extended so far as to simply stand over them until breathing implied consciousness.

The Dremora, he informed them, would be waking soon. And they should speak with the Vestige, who waited outside its door.

They found the Crow Father exactly where promised — seated upon a broken block, shuffling a deck of cards with the unhurried ease of a man who had waited out longer sieges than this one. He could not go in there himself, he explained, with a dry lightness that carried no lightness at all.

He was the Scourge of Coldharbour. No Dremora would endure questioning at his hands — Daedra faced with him did not talk; they shut their mouths and died hoping to escape him that way. So the questioning fell to Onasha and Periwyn.

But not without assistance.

Within the chamber, laid out upon the table with the ceremony of a surgeon arranging instruments, waited the Vestige's own weapons. A set of small ebony daggers, their edges fine enough to part Daedric plate like wet parchment. And a malachite dueling sword, so nearly weightless in the hand that it felt less like a blade than a thought with an edge.

Far better suited, he noted, to the delicacies of interrogation than anything the two of them carried.

As for the Dremora's retaliation — that they need not fear. The Vestige possessed another trick. Not up his sleeve.

Upon his finger.

A ring, enchanted by a grandmaster of the craft: a spell of silence shaped for Dremora and their magics alone. However the creature strained, however it cursed or clawed at the threads of its power, no spell would leave its hands. It would be a mortal thing, in a mortal's cage.

He gave his instructions quietly and without cruelty, the way one briefs soldiers.

Onasha, for her part, was plainly still shaken. The dungeon beneath Kjallgrad had reopened wounds that no night by a frozen pond had fully closed, and shackles were not so easily forgotten. She avoided even gazing at the Crow Father, and he — who noticed everything — matched her discretion by turning his explanation almost entirely toward Periwyn, granting her distance as though it were a courtesy between equals.

Sometimes the kindest thing a legend can do is look elsewhere.

---

The Dremora was lucid when they entered — or near enough to it. Periwyn made certain of the distinction, waking it loudly, with a boot and a voice, on the principle that confusion was a luxury captives did not deserve.

The questions were simple. Straightforward, even.

Their captive proved difficult regardless.

Despite the cuts. Despite the daggers standing in it like fence-posts. Despite the exquisite agony of the enchantments eating at it from within — it hurled insults with the relish of a creature that had outlived every consequence. It answered vaguely. It answered with questions of its own. It answered, at times, in verse, apparently for its own amusement.

But one remark found its mark.

_"If my master had it his way,"_ it said, grinning through its ruined composure, _"you would all be slaves to us for all eternity — bound to our servitude, like the insignificant mortals you are."_

Onasha froze.

Just briefly. A single heartbeat, visible only to those who knew to look.

Then she turned and walked out of the room without a word.

The Dremora smiled, slow and satisfied, believing it had finally won something.

It was mistaken.

For behind those amber eyes, a plan had already been conceived — sudden, reckless, and brilliant, the kind of plan that arrives whole, like lightning finding the tallest tree. Some would even call it a plan, Onasha would later concede, on the grounds that the word had certain obligations attached.

She marched into the adjoining chamber — and stopped short, startled, at the sight of the Vestige seated quietly by the door, playing solitaire.

She recovered, and spoke with the assassin.

Her need was simple. And perhaps difficult.

A life.

He would travel deep into Morrowind — far into the lands of House Dres — and kill a single Dunmer. One rather personal to Onasha.

The slave-driver who had murdered her mother.

His name was Delos Nerethi.

She had neither the time nor the reagents to perform a Black Sacrament, and so a simple contract would have to serve. She paid the gold up front, counted into the assassin's palm without ceremony, as though she had rehearsed the moment all her life.

On her way back to the interrogation room, she passed the Vestige once more.

He still sat with his solitaire spread before him, patient as the mountains.

She kicked over his cards.

It did not amuse the poor mer.

---

Inside, while Periwyn amused herself by idly testing which of the embedded daggers the Dremora could tolerate — a game it was losing — Onasha carried out the second part of her design.

She looked the creature in the eyes, and offered it a contract.

The terms were simple. She spoke them slowly, so there could be no misunderstanding.

Onasha would let it leave the ruin unharmed — by her hand, or Periwyn's. She would see to it that the Camonna Tong agents who had failed it were dealt with.

In exchange, the Dremora would answer any and all questions put to it, truthfully and to the best of its considerable knowledge.

And it would kill Delos Nerethi.

The Dremora weighed the offer with the swagger of a creature that had never once been outmaneuvered by anything it could still see. It found the terms agreeable. Rather generous, even. It was far too arrogant to notice the holes in the writing — the places where a careful reader might have asked _unharmed by whom, precisely?_, or paused at the breadth of _any and all questions_.

They signed it in blood.

Periwyn, with a cruelty that suggested genuine promise, suggested they use one of the daggers still lodged in the Dremora's flesh as the pen. Onasha concurred.

The contract flared once — blue flame, cold and hungry — and was gone, written now upon a ledger older than any of them.

Its most damning clause was also its quietest: should either party fail to complete their side of the agreement, or violate its terms in any way, their soul would be forfeit to the other.

The Dremora, flush with the certainty of its own cleverness, did not so much as blink.

Then it began to talk.

And the answers, when they came, were more than substantial — though not quite enough to reveal the whole of the picture.

Of Kjallgrad, the image was clear. Flood the city with brimstone coin — cursed gold that would pass from hand to hand like a contagion. Then, with a single ritual, make it vanish. Every coin, every ledger, every saving and debt, erased at once. And watch as the city tore itself apart — neighbours turned against neighbours, the Silver Road collapsing into ruin, its trade strangled in a night. And when the people had exhausted their despair, the only hands extended to lift them would belong to the Cult of Clavicus Vile.

Salvation, purchased at the usual price.

Of the powers above, the picture grew murky. The Dremora knew titles, not truths. The Jarl of Kjallgrad, as the party had long suspected, was no Jarl at all — the seat had been taken by one of its own kind, a Dremora wearing the true Jarl's memories like a stolen coat, while the man himself rotted in endless torture at the hands of Molag Bal.

And above even that, their immediate superior was known only by one title.

The Dread Lady.

Of the Coiled Serpent, the Coiled Path — frustratingly, the creature had heard nothing. But with Vaelin so deeply entrenched in the Silver Road, Onasha and Periwyn could not help but feel the threads were somehow the same rope.

When the pair emerged and recounted everything, the Vestige listened without interruption.

His expression, when they finished, was a rare thing: both astonished and disappointed.

He had felt the contract's magic from outside the room — had felt it seal, and understood at once what had been signed, and upon what terms. Onasha's plan, he said, had been reckless. Stupid. Mad.

And it had worked.

It made her, he noted, only the second person he had ever seen outsmart a Dremora by exploiting its arrogance — a distinction he delivered with the tone of a man reluctantly adding a name to a very short list.

Then he turned to practicalities. They would rest. They would gather their strength and stock themselves with whatever the ruin's larders and their own reserves allowed.

For at dusk, they would return to the Altan Mesa.

To deal with what remained of Vile's Cult, once and for all.

And to stop Kjallgrad from its imminent collapse.
