## Chapter 23 — The Scent of Coin

**Morndas, 1st of Sun’s Dusk, 2E 600**

The morning arrived warmer than any Kjallgrad had seen in weeks.

The frost had not yet surrendered entirely — thin sheets still clung stubbornly to window sills and shaded stones — but the air carried the faint promise that winter’s grip could loosen, if only for a while.

Periwyn woke early as she often did and went directly to the stables. Ashen greeted her with the quiet familiarity of routine, and after feeding the mare she settled nearby with a small stack of tomes purchased over the past days. Instead of sketching, she studied. Lines of ink replaced lines of charcoal, and the stable’s quiet proved a suitable place for both horse and learning.

Upstairs, Onasha began her morning differently.

One by one she removed several wooden panels that had covered the doors and windows since their arrival. The sound of wood shifting against frame and wall carried through the room, stirring Kirya awake before long. Though surprised at first, Kirya joined her without complaint, and together they worked until the room allowed daylight again.

The caution that had once demanded sealed windows had begun to loosen.

When the work was finished, they descended toward the stables. There they found Periwyn already absorbed in her reading, while nearby a pair of stableboys spoke in low, irritated voices about matters that had begun troubling the district.

The Guild of the Silver Road had raised tariffs sharply over the last few days.

The increase had not gone unnoticed. Several merchants who had spoken against the decision had reportedly been arrested, taken quietly by guards who seemed less interested in law than instruction. One of the boys mentioned a clothing merchant taken the previous day — a man whose moustache and elaborate fashion sense had made him easy to recognize in the market.

Onasha remembered him.

When she asked why the people did nothing, the answer came with the weary practicality of those accustomed to surviving under heavier boots. Too many guards were paid by the guild. Few were willing to die simply to make a point.

Still, whispers had begun to circulate. Certain figures — not known for honest dealings — had started asking questions among the warehouses, seeking out workers and listening to their grievances.

It was then that Kirya noticed the figure watching them from the treeline.

Recognition came quickly: the Vestige.

The three followed immediately, moving toward the woods in hopes of catching him before he vanished again into whatever paths he favored. Yet the trail did not last long. His footprints dissolved into scattered tracks among the brush, leaving only uncertainty behind.

On the walk back, Periwyn paused to demonstrate a new piece of her studies.

From her outstretched hand burst a narrow beam of blazing light — bright and steady like concentrated sunlight. It scorched a nearby patch of snow into steam before fading again, leaving the air faintly warm in its wake.

The display was brief, but impressive enough to linger in memory.

Durov, meanwhile, had woken later than usual.

The morning greeted him with a lingering heaviness that made the descent downstairs slower than normal. When he entered the inn’s common room he noticed a familiar figure already seated in the corner with a mug of mead: Captain Irlav.

The captain had been waiting.

Both had received the same assignment for the day: oversee the transfer of provisions between warehouses in the district and ensure that nothing disturbed the process. The job sounded routine, though recent tensions suggested otherwise.

They spoke briefly before leaving together toward the warehouse quarter.

By the time Kirya, Onasha, and Periwyn returned from the woods, Durov had already gone. None of them questioned the absence. The city offered too many errands to track every movement.

Instead, they turned toward their own task — a scheduled meeting with Vaelin Marr. Along the way they paused in the market to purchase a small bundle of fruit before continuing to the Silver Road guildhouse.

Elsewhere, Durov and Irlav encountered trouble before they even reached their destination.

A crowd had gathered around a city guard, voices raised in anger over rising food prices. Tariffs had already begun tightening the markets, and frustration now spilled openly into the streets.

When a stone flew toward the guard, Durov intercepted it with a raised arm before it struck.

Rather than escalate, he stepped forward and addressed the crowd. His words were calm and steady. He acknowledged their grievances openly — the prices, the arrests, the strain now visible in every market stall — yet reminded them that violence would only give the guild reason to tighten its grip further.

He promised to look into the disappearances personally and urged them to bring their complaints through proper channels once he found them.

Gradually the anger dissolved into murmurs. The crowd dispersed, not satisfied, but no longer on the edge of riot.

By the time Durov and Irlav reached Warehouse Seven, the morning had settled into labor.

Most crates proved unremarkable: grain, furs, preserved provisions. Yet some stood out immediately — heavier than expected, sealed with wax sigils that did not match the usual marks. When opened, their contents revealed stranger cargo: coin molds matching the guild’s so-called new minting process, gemstones, chalk, black candles, and bundles of animal remains — bone, sinew, and pig hearts wrapped in cloth.

The guard overseeing the shipment insisted the items appeared on official paperwork. Yet the caravan driver’s own ledger told a different story.

Ignoring the guard’s protests, Durov and Irlav ordered the suspicious crates unloaded. Without a supervising officer present, they refused to allow the shipment to continue.

Later, when workers were paid for their labor, another problem surfaced.

The coin smelled faintly of brimstone.

One caravan master refused the payment outright. To avoid further confrontation, Durov paid the man with his own conventional coin and kept the questionable pieces instead.

Tension returned when a guard, already irritated by delays, shoved one of the workers to the ground during an argument over the gold. The man struck his head against the stone floor.

While Irlav restrained the guard, Durov tended to the injured worker.

Grateful, the man offered a quiet observation. The strange smell of the coin reminded him of the anchors from the war — the Planemeld, when Molag Bal had cast Mooring Anchors across Tamriel.

Another worker approached afterward and thanked Durov for intervening. His words carried a hint of familiarity — a suggestion that some of the city’s less reputable circles had begun organizing opposition to the guild’s influence.

Across the city, the others had arrived at the Silver Road guildhouse.

Guards checked their identification before escorting them through the building’s stone halls to Vaelin’s office, which overlooked several districts of the city. Periwyn played her part as planned: an eager young merchant hoping to restore her family’s trading company after years away.

The performance worked.

Vaelin eventually produced a draft agreement for the Lunclaire Company. Under its terms, the company would pay an eight percent tariff rather than the standard fifteen in exchange for valuable trade routes through the Rift and beyond, a permanent warehouse in southeastern Skyrim, faster escorted passage, and fewer inspections.

In return, the guild would occasionally request transport of its cargo and reports concerning the movements of competing merchants.

Vaelin described his employers as an organization that ensured merchants had a voice in politics — one with connections reaching royal courts and mercenary companies alike.

Before negotiations could deepen further, a guard entered with urgent news of unrest near the warehouses.

Vaelin excused himself to address the matter, leaving the three alone in his office.

They did not waste the opportunity.

Onasha immediately began examining the room’s documents, relying on memory rather than theft. A map on the wall displayed two marked circles within the warehouse district and a third location northeast of the city. A ledger on the desk recorded recent transfers: shipments between warehouses, references to a “special consignment” allocated to a temple that did not exist in Kjallgrad, and entries describing the circulation of newly minted coin.

Kirya proved equally efficient. Within moments she had opened both a cabinet and a lockbox.

Inside were partially burned letters referencing supervision of the Jarl’s condition, contracts from the Silver Road, evidence of bribes paid to officials in Riften, and fragments discussing something called _the coming correction_.

Once Onasha had memorized the contents, everything was returned precisely as it had been.

Vaelin returned shortly afterward, mildly irritated. Workers, he remarked, had chosen that day to remember they possessed opinions.

Periwyn steered the conversation briefly toward the pendant he wore — an ouroboros symbol hanging openly at his chest. Vaelin explained that it had been given to survivors of the assault on the White-Gold Tower during the Planemeld, representing the three alliances that had fought in the war.

The explanation sounded plausible enough to satisfy casual curiosity.

Soon after, the meeting ended amicably and the trio were escorted from the guildhouse.

By early afternoon they had all reunited at the inn, sharing what each had learned. Patterns began forming quickly: the suspicious shipments, the ledger entries, the marked location outside the city.

Onasha proposed a new plan.

By using disguises and a few carefully placed explosives, they might provoke conflict between the Silver Road and the Camonna Tong. She revealed that she had already fed the Tong false information suggesting Vaelin possessed a lockbox they were seeking.

The trap, if sprung correctly, might expose far more than either side intended.

Before they could disperse for the evening, a familiar visitor arrived at the window.

The Knave of Rooks landed lightly on the sill and delivered a small message from its unseen master.

The note was brief, a riddle of sorts, with an addendum written in careful script:

_Seek the knowledge of dust and dried ink._

The meaning was unclear.

But in Kjallgrad, where coin smelled of brimstone and ledgers concealed more than numbers, such words rarely arrived without reason.
