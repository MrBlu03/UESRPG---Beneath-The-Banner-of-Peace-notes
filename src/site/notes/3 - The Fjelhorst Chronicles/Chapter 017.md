## Chapter  17 – The Hunt at Winter's End

**8th of Sun’s Dusk, 2E 600**

Morning came brittle and cold, the air glazed with frost that clung to breath and blade alike. The Fangs rose in silence, each tending to their rituals as though in prayer. Periwyn moved first, her hands sure and methodical as she inspected her sword, her warhammer, each clasp and knot of her gear. Every motion spoke of quiet purpose — a knight preparing not merely for battle, but for reckoning.

Kirya rose soon after, laying out her arrows in ordered rows. She dipped each point into the flame varnish prepared the day before, the faint scent of oil and smoke lingering in the air. Once finished, she roasted a few small fish for them — a humble meal for what might be their last. Onasha checked her bowstring and leather bindings, movements efficient and spare. Durov, slower than usual and marked by fatigue, donned his armor in silence before joining them, eating without a word.

When the Hunter’s Bell tolled, the sound rolled through the morning mist like a funeral chant. They stepped into the square beneath a sky of pewter gray. The pyres from the previous night still burned low, and the smell of their smoke clung to every cloak and hood. Svala awaited them beneath the palisade, the rope-burns on her hands now deep and raw. Around her stood the others: Torik with woad streaked across his face, Ingrin taut with nervous resolve, Runa and Alma testing their bowstrings one last time.

The woad ritual began again. Torik’s brush traced the blue pigment over each brow and cheek, his motions deliberate. As Onasha assisted, she quietly inquired about other ways to wound the Ragged Lord beyond flame. Torik’s tone grew grave — the creature’s hides, he explained, were not trophies but extensions of its being. To destroy them was to strike at its essence. None before had faced it and lived. Today would be the first trial. Its claim upon the forest, its so-called inheritance, would be broken.

By midmorning, the woods had swallowed them whole. The path they knew had warped overnight — snow deeper, air denser, a humming stillness that pressed against the skin like unseen breath. The sky itself was shrouded, heavy clouds muting the world into shadow.

From the treeline came the shape of wolves — too distant to engage, too close to mistake as chance. Their eyes glowed faintly in the dim light, reflecting more thought than hunger. As the Fangs pressed on, they found new totems hanging from the branches. Some bore faces they recognized — Bjorr’s among them. Others twisted toward likenesses of their own, faint but undeniable. Periwyn’s scar etched perfectly in sinew, Kirya’s braid of flax hair, Onasha’s sharp jawline. Durov’s image remained faceless beneath a carved helm; the creature did not know the man within the iron.

Torik examined the carvings, theorizing that the totems embodied the Ragged Lord’s claim over all who walked its land — inheritance by blood and trespass alike.

Every step stirred whispers from unseen mouths — too low to discern, too many to count. Fear crept slow and inevitable into the company. Runa began to murmur invocations to Kyne. Ingrin’s breathing turned shallow, trembling. Periwyn and Onasha flanked her wordlessly, guiding her forward, anchoring her focus to the rhythm of their footsteps.

The totems grew thicker as they advanced. Each one bled faintly from the mouth, steam rising from the wounds. The scent of iron filled the air. Then, at last, the trees broke — and before them lay the clearing once more.

The shrine had changed. The hides now draped the canopy like a second sky, stitched into a ghastly dome. Bones ringed the clearing in uneven circles, their frost-fused edges catching the wan light. At the center pulsed a dim glow — the heart of the shrine, throbbing like a wound that refused to close.

They gathered before it in silence. Torik laid out his plan. The Ragged Lord, for all its malice, was still a hunter, one driven by instinct and pattern. It would not face them by choice. He had uncovered its nymic — the true name that bound its being. By speaking it through ritual, he could force it from its veil, draw it into form. The act would not go unanswered. The creature’s beasts would come first, and the Fangs would need to hold the circle while he completed the rite.

---

The clearing held its breath.

There was no wind, no birdcall — only the faint hiss of Torik’s brazier and the rhythm of his murmured words threading through the stillness. He knelt before the shrine’s heart, knife in hand, tracing runes into the frost. Each cut left a line of dull ember-light glowing beneath the snow.

The words he spoke were old, older than the Eight, older than Ysgramor’s line — syllables that belonged to the marrow of the world. Frost crept across the brazier, turning its blue flame to white as his voice deepened, invoking names not heard in mortal halls. The ground trembled.

Then the flame screamed. A high, metallic wail split the air — and from far beyond the treeline, a howl rose to answer. Low at first, then joined by another, and another, until the forest itself seemed to breathe in unison with the sound.

From the shadows stepped the first wolf — its eyes glimmering with pale green light, its fur bristling with frost. Then another emerged. Then five more. They came without sound, save for the wet, rhythmic crunch of snow beneath their paws.

The hunt had begun.

Kirya moved first. With a wordless growl, she drew and loosed in one fluid motion. Two arrows found their mark — the first tearing through fur and bone, the second burying itself deep in the wolf’s throat. It collapsed without a sound.

Alma took aim beside her. Her first arrow vanished into the dark, but her second struck true, spinning a wolf aside mid-leap. Svala’s bowstring sang next — one shot missing wide, the other piercing the wounded beast’s skull, felling it before it reached them.

The clearing flickered with lightning. Periwyn thrust out her hand, a crack of white fire arcing through the air, splitting into twin bolts — both missing their marks as the wolves darted through the stormlight.

Onasha stood still, paralyzed by the sudden ferocity. Her fingers twitched at her bow, but the memory of the Ragged Woods’ whispers held her frozen.

Runa broke to the flank, firing two quick arrows. Both flew wide — the wolves were too fast, their movements unnatural, blurring with every step.

The beasts circled, silent as breath. Frost mist rose around them, their forms little more than phantoms in the white haze.

Ingrin’s arrow whistled past another wolf’s muzzle. It ducked, snow scattering. Then Durov advanced — steel meeting motion. His sword flashed, cleaving through one wolf’s snout in a single brutal swing. The creature fell twitching, black blood hissing in the snow. Durov stepped back, guarding Torik’s chanting form.

But another wolf lunged, teeth bared, aiming straight for the kneeling priest.

Kirya fired — missed. Svala’s next arrow struck a wolf farther out, not the one charging. Alma, steadying her breath, loosed two arrows in quick succession. Both found the onrushing beast — one in the flank, the other deep in the ribs. The wolf crumpled mere breaths from Torik’s circle.

Periwyn met another head-on. Her blade flashed low — severing its forelegs cleanly. As it tumbled, she drove her sword through its skull, pinning it to the ground.

Runa’s next arrow hit true, impaling another wolf through the side, but it staggered on, snarling. Onasha moved at last. Her arrow struck the creature through the snout, the tip bursting from the back of its skull. Without pause, she loosed another, striking a second wolf in the ribs.

That wolf turned on Runa, clamping its jaws around his leg, dragging him toward the trees. Snow churned red.

Ingrin roared, sword flashing. She brought it down in a clean, brutal arc — the blade shearing through fur and bone alike. The wolf’s head hit the snow, rolling to stillness.

The clearing went silent save for the hiss of blood on frost. The last wolf twitched once, then lay still.

---

The moment of calm was brief. Torik staggered to his feet, the frost crawling up his arms like veins of ice. His voice deepened — layered, resonant, no longer solely his own. The runes carved into the snow flared green and white, pulsing like the heartbeat of something ancient.

*“Veit ek þik, veiðimaðr villr. Veit ek þik, Nohrak, flǫkna herra Kyne!”*

The brazier shattered, hurling ash and fire skyward. The forest groaned in reply. From the branches above, hides tore free and spiraled through the air like wings of smoke.

Then the treeline darkened.

He stepped forth — the Ragged Lord.

A towering shape of sinew and hide, antlers black as char. Faces stretched across his body like trapped souls, their mouths moving soundlessly beneath the translucent flesh. Steam poured from seams in his form, rising as a cold fog. When he tilted his stag-skull head, the antlers cracked like breaking wood.

His voice came not as a sound, but a chorus of whispers — a word spoken by a dozen stolen tongues:

*“Hunter.”*

---

The world blinked.

In an instant, Nohrak was behind Alma, mirroring her stance as though mocking her very motion. His claws — each a foot of glacial bone — slashed downward. Alma fell with a cry, blood splattering the altar stones.

Through sheer will, she raised her sword, its edge burning with the varnished fire Torik had brewed. She struck upward — the blade biting deep into one of the creature’s hides. Nohrak howled, the wound searing black as the fire took hold. He vanished into mist, reappearing a dozen paces behind them.

Kirya drew and fired — one arrow burying shallowly in his chest, the other missing as Nohrak tilted his skull aside. Alma crawled behind the altar, clutching her wound, and drank from one of Torik’s crimson vials. The bleeding slowed.

Svala turned, spotting movement in the trees — three more wolves emerging from the fog. She fired twice, one arrow punching clean through a wolf’s eye, felling it instantly.

Runa rose, limping but resolute. His arrow struck Nohrak in the arm. The creature recoiled, snarling. Onasha’s next shot pierced his knee, forcing him to stagger. His head snapped toward her, and his scream split the clearing — a sound that warped the air itself.

Onasha sprinted toward Alma, binding her wound with shaking hands.

Torik, his ritual complete, lifted a trembling palm. A sphere of fire erupted from his hand, slamming into Nohrak’s chest. The beast staggered, flames licking across his body. Torik turned, laying a healing hand upon Runa, the light of Kyne soft against the hunter’s torn leg.

Ingrin caught sight of another wolf charging Svala. She fired — the arrow struck home, dropping it mid-leap. The last wolf scraped past, grazing Svala’s arm but little more.

Nohrak, enraged, appeared again — this time before Durov and Periwyn. His claws came down in a blur. Durov shoved Periwyn aside, raising his shield. The blow punched through the metal, slicing into his arm. Gritting his teeth, he struck back, his sword cleaving into Nohrak’s leg. The Daedra fell to one knee as the fire took him, his flesh cracking under its heat.

---

The Ragged Lord screamed — a sound that was not one voice, but many: man, beast, and child all at once. Flames consumed his form, hides tearing loose and whipping through the air like burning banners.

Periwyn slashed one aside as Durov raised his shield, deflecting the rest. What emerged from the conflagration was leaner, skeletal — sinew stretched over bone, frost steaming from each joint. The stag’s skull had fused to its face, antlers glowing a molten orange from the fire within. His breath came heavy through the stag’s nostrils — each exhale a gust of freezing air.

He lunged again, the remnants of his hides coiling about one arm like a whip. Periwyn parried, steel meeting hide with a sound like cracking ice.

Svala loosed another arrow, striking the last wolf still standing. It whimpered, staggered — but did not fall.

Periwyn saw her moment. She stepped in close, blade flashing in an arc that cleaved through Nohrak’s right arm, then cut deep into his left. The limbs fell, smoldering.

Onasha took position to the flank, drawing a single arrow. She exhaled — and let it fly. It struck the same wound she had made before, shattering the creature’s knee entirely. Nohrak collapsed, crashing to the snow.

His stag skull split down the center with a sharp crack. Beneath it lay a human face — burned, scarred, hollow-eyed. His voice now singular, he rasped:

*“End it.”*

Periwyn stepped forward. She raised her longsword and drove it through his ribs. The blade met no resistance. Nohrak’s hand rose, grasped the weapon — and he pushed himself deeper onto it.

His final word came as a whisper, faint and cold as the falling snow:

*“Hunter.”*

Then his body burned away. The hides collapsed into ash. The frost in the air melted into mist, and the forest exhaled — long, deep, and still.

The Hunt was over.

---

The woods were silent once more. No whispers. No groaning of bone or rustle of hides. Only wind—soft, clean, and true—threading between the trees like a sigh of relief. Smoke still rose from the clearing, thin and white, curling into the pale afternoon sky. The clouds parted above, and sunlight fell upon the hollow where the Ragged Lord had burned.

The frost that had coated the earth began to melt, revealing soil dark and rich beneath. The scent of birch sap and ash lingered—sharp, alive, cleansing. Somewhere high above, a hawk cried. For the first time in many days, no wolf answered.

Onasha and Kirya, wordless and weary, followed the rhythm of that quiet. They gathered what the hunt had left behind—wolf pelts for trade, hearts for alchemy, meat enough to last the road home. Their movements were steady, reverent almost, as though honoring what had been taken.

When the work was done, they turned their steps back toward Faldarstad, helping Alma to her feet. She leaned against Periwyn for balance, her breath still uneven, her eyes distant with pain and exhaustion. Torik walked beside them, arms folded tight against the cold. He murmured that he would write of this—every word, every sign, every wound—so that those who came after would know how to face such darkness should it rise again.

The Ragged Woods no longer pressed against them. The air moved freely, alive with the chatter of unseen birds. The totems that had once wept and whispered were gone; only ash and melted snow remained. Each step seemed lighter than the last. The forest breathed again, and with it came the quiet pulse of Kyne’s presence—a sense of watching, not of menace, but of mercy.

By the time the city’s watchtowers appeared through the frost-mist, the sun was sinking low. Amber and red light poured over the Treva River, turning it to molten gold. The gates of Faldarstad stood open. The bell at the Hunter’s Shrine tolled once, sharp and clear across the water.

As they entered the town, faces emerged from doorways and market stalls—pale, expectant, unbelieving. One by one, the people followed, forming a slow tide behind the weary hunters. At the market steps, young Hoki stood waiting, clutching a carved wooden stag. When he saw Svala, he smiled through tears, and the Fangs paused long enough to reassure him before moving on.

Then the bell tolled again—answered by horns from the walls, the beat of drums, the echo of laughter. For the first time in weeks, Faldarstad sounded alive.

From within the crowd came a figure broad as a bear, beard like a snowdrift, his huscarl Helga Cairn-Shield beside him, hand on sword-hilt. Jarl Gorm of Faldarstad. His gratitude was plain; his voice broke with pride as he thanked them. He paid them richly, swearing that the house of Nielan would remain theirs whenever they returned. Helga offered a quiet nod to Svala—thanks for bringing her sister back from the woods.

That night, the town burned bright with celebration. Torches and banners lined the square, mead flowed like riverwater, and roasted venison crackled over open fires. Songs rose to meet the aurora, which returned in ribbons of gold and violet that danced above the rooftops.

Periwyn sat apart, sketching the moment by firelight—children darting through the snow in wooden masks shaped like stags and hawks. In the square’s center, a new totem stood: Kyne’s Hawk, wings outstretched to the stars. Beneath it, Svala and Torik lit incense, not from fear, but in gratitude.

Kirya joined the music, her lute rising above the crowd in a song of the Hunt and its end—a melody both mourning and triumphant.

When the revelry faded, Onasha found Torik leaning against the wall of the Soggy Beaver, the aurora’s light caught in his eyes. In his hand he held a small charm—an antler carved into the shape of a running stag. He pressed it into her palm. _Even the gods can lose their way,_ he said softly, _and still be found._

Later, Periwyn and Onasha sat together outside the house. They spoke quietly, their words light as snow. Periwyn mentioned, almost absently, that she had not dreamed since the coma—that for the first time, her sleep had been still.

Out of the settling dark came Stryder—hooded, unreadable, yet smiling faintly. He offered his congratulations and vanished again into the night’s hum, leaving only the faint scent of oil and ink in his wake.

When they finally returned to Nielan’s house, the quiet that greeted them was deep and warm. No wind, no whispers, only the faint crackle of the hearth and the steady rhythm of their own breaths.

The dread of the Ragged Woods had lifted.

And for the first time since their arrival in the Rift, the Fangs of Fjelhorst slept in peace.
