## Chapter 30 – The Price of a Promise

**Fredas, 12th of Evening Star, 2E 600 (afternoon)**

The second camp was smaller than the first, and angrier for it.

Durov, Captain Irlav, and Proventus found it nestled in a fold of hills southeast of the Altan Mesa — a scatter of tents barely held together, a fire burning low and unattended, not a single stake or palisade raised against the world. Whoever had fled here had done so without plan, without time, and without much hope.

The three reined in on a small cliff overlooking the camp, arriving from the northwest, and took stock. Only Proventus carried a bow. He dismounted without being told and settled onto the ridge, arrow nocked, while Durov and Irlav slipped down and around, working through the trees to the south.

The Camonna Tong did not see them coming.

Durov reached the nearest sentry from behind, and his silver great axe came down in a single, terrible arc. The man was nearly cloven in half. Such a death could not be quiet — a blow of that weight landing on an ordinary body would have needed divine intervention to pass in silence — and the sound of it did its work, freezing the camp in the moment between hearing and understanding.

It was all the opening they required.

From the ridge, Proventus loosed arrow after arrow, forcing the remaining Tong to keep low, to dive for cover, to watch the treeline instead of their own flanks. In that stolen blindness, Durov and Irlav moved through the camp like a single efficient machine — weaving between tents, striking where the cover of canvas hid them, and leaving silence in their wake.

But one tent gave up a surprise.

A battlemage emerged hurling flame.

Durov took the brunt of it — blow after searing blow across his guard and cloak, the leather beginning to smoke and curl as he cleared the men around the mage. At length he cast aside the burning cloak, drew sword and shield, and closed the gap before the mage could reset. As the two collided, Proventus' arrow hissed in from the ridge, forcing the mage to duck — and in that instant, Durov lunged, driving his blade deep into the man's gut.

The mage tried to answer with fire one last time.

Durov saw it coming.

He caught the mage's outstretched spell-hand in his own, and wedged it — still burning, still gathering its power — into the wounded abdomen before him.

The spell fired all the same.

It liquified the poor fool's chest from the inside, and he collapsed with the strange, unfinished expression of a man whose last act had been his own undoing.

---

When it was done, they took stock of the camp, and found it as barren as the first.

No correspondence. No letters. No ledger. Nothing that pointed anywhere but back toward the camp they had already destroyed. Whoever these Tong had answered to, they had carried their orders in their heads and nowhere else.

The trio looted what they could — weapons, armor, whatever provisions had survived the flight — and made their way back to the Altan Mesa.

By the time they arrived and handed in their report, Lord Marr was already preparing to leave.

Irlav protested.

It was, Durov thought, the most spine he had seen the captain show since the night they first rode out together. Marr's people — the excavation crews, the camp servants, the families that had grown up in the shadow of the brass doors — would be left behind to fend for themselves amid the collapse that was coming for Kjallgrad. Irlav did not wish to abandon them to it.

Lord Marr heard him out with something like patience.

Then he asked, very quietly, whether Irlav — and *his family* — truly supported such a stance.

The words were not loud.

They did not need to be.

Irlav backed down, begrudgingly, and the withdrawal continued as though no objection had ever been raised.

As they made ready to depart, Durov pulled the captain aside. He had seen the threat land, and had marked it for what it was.

Irlav explained, with the flat voice of a man reciting a wound: Lord Marr held his family somewhere in the mountains. He had not seen them in years. He did not know, in any given week, whether they still lived — only that Marr's silence on the matter was a promise of what would happen if he stepped out of line again.

Durov swore, there and then, that he would find anything he could on where they were kept — and that Irlav should hold strong through what was coming.

They shook hands.

Two soldiers, parting in the hope of better times.

Then the captain rode out with the withdrawal, and Durov turned back toward the interior of the ruin — toward the smithing chamber, and the long, patient work of smelting down everything they had taken from the dead.

---

**Back at the Tomb of the Oath-Breaker King**

While the ruin's fires burned in the north, other lessons were underway in the south.

Periwyn sat cross-legged in the main chamber, the Tome open before her, prepared bones laid out in a careful row. The lesson for the day: break bone, channel it, and hurl it forward like shrapnel.

She tried.

Then tried again.

Then again.

With each failure her agitation mounted, and the bones — sensitive things, attuned to the intentions that moved them — grew only more resistant as her anger rose. The harder she reached, the further they slipped from her grasp. It was an ugly loop, and she fell into it completely.

At last she decided the bones themselves were the problem.

She seized a different one, cracked it, and poured magicka into the fracture.

It worked.

The shards rose, singing — and, in her rage, she hurled them toward the far end of the chamber, where the Vestige sat with his back turned, ostensibly minding his own business.

They whizzed past his face, close enough to part the air beside his ear.

Without even turning around, he chuckled.

_"Your aim is terrible,"_ he observed. _"My head was slightly more to the right."_

---

The next lesson would require somewhere the dead had not found rest — and there were few such places left in this corner of the Rift. Periwyn thought of the Widow's River, where an old Jarl's wife had walked into the water to drown herself and her grief together. But the last time they had passed that way, a Wispmother had haunted the site, and she had no wish to fight spirits while her hands were full of bones and doctrine.

So she asked Onasha for help.

Onasha agreed without hesitation, and pressed a healing elixir into her hand before they left — freshly prepared, just in case.

Together they made their way through the forest once more, past the Three Stone Brothers, along the path beside the frozen lake, until they reached the ground the widow had chosen for her ending.

Periwyn knelt, dug her bare hands into the frozen soil, and reached.

The past was there, waiting.

She pulled at the widow's final moments — and it came flooding up through the earth like water through a broken dam. Grief. Immense, total grief. The grief of losing someone to an unknown fate, of a love with nowhere left to go. It burrowed into every corner of Periwyn's psyche, seeking out every buried thing she had ever locked away—

And then it was snuffed out. Destroyed. Consumed by something colder and more willful than itself: pure agony. The agony of drowning, willingly, in winter water, for the chance to meet her husband one last time.

The two griefs met, and each destroyed the other.

Periwyn tried to let it go. She told herself that the widow had died of her own free will, that this had been a choice, that there was nothing to carry home from it. But her own grief rose to answer the widow's — her brother, her family, her temple — and it came flooding back all at once, undamned and unstoppable.

Periwyn sat motionless at the lake's edge, tears streaming silently down her cheeks, staring at nothing.

For over an hour.

Eventually Onasha knelt in front of her — close enough to be present, far enough to respect whatever distance grief demanded — and asked whether she was alright. Whether things were still going as they should.

Periwyn's eyes, at last, refocused.

And together they headed back toward the tomb.

---

On the walk back, Onasha pressed the question that had been sitting with her since the night by the pond.

Necromancy. Could it actually bring someone back — wholly, with their memories, as they had been?

For how could it be otherwise? Among her people — the Black-Tongue Tribe — to deny Sithis in such a way was blasphemy. Sacrilege. The worst crime they could commit. The dead belonged to the void, and to claw them back from it was to unmake the natural order itself.

And even setting faith aside — since Periwyn did not share hers — there was only one instance, in all the histories either of them knew, of a mortal being brought back as he had been.

The Vestige himself.

And that, Onasha said, made him no longer a man. Whatever else he had been, whatever he still carried, he was — for all intents and purposes — a Daedra now.

Periwyn did not argue.

She could not.

Because to her, it simply had to work. It *would* work.

Because what other option did she have?

What she did not expect was for the Tome itself to answer.

The book shook in her hands — glowing dimly in the afternoon light, pulling slightly against her grip, as though leaning away from her her rather than towards. And she understood, with the certainty of someone reading a language older than words, that the knowledge within came with a condition.

It would teach her.

But only for the right reasons.

---

**Kjallgrad, later that afternoon**

Onasha and Periwyn gathered their effects, made ready, and slipped back toward Kjallgrad to collect the priest they had hired.

Still believing themselves fugitives, they entered through the old sewers — and found themselves, once again, in Tarja's hideout.

Much had changed since their last visit.

The people, Tarja reported, were growing bolder. Some guards had turned violent during the earlier protest, and the violence had killed citizens — a few of them, at least, dead in the streets where the crowd had stood. The Jarl and the steward were presumed dead or missing, the hall having burned with no one accounted for. The Black-Foot Merchant's Guild was buying up Silver Road holdings through various vague and dubious legal charters, swallowing the city one document at a time.

And Tarja's people — the vermin, the forgotten, the ones nobody counted — were waiting for the right opportunity to strike at the Guild and end it for good.

Whether that strike would be peaceful or violent, Tarja could not say.

That, she explained, depended on the people above — on the ordinary citizen, and on his aptitude for abrupt change.

When Onasha asked about reaching Frostgate Hall without being spotted, Tarja pointed to an exit on the map of the sewers — one that would let them out only a few streets from the inn. She handed Onasha the map itself, having already given her first copy away to the refugees from the warehouse in the woods.

They left to find the priest.

The sewers eventually led them to the basement of an old house, where some of Tarja's men waited. Among them stood Delvin — the same vermin who had helped them burn the warehouses — and beneath a cloth cover on the table before him, jars of oil. Many jars. They were saving those for the Guild house.

An eventuality, Delvin said, for another time.

---

At Frostgate Hall, they met with the unnamed Priest of Stendarr, and apologized for the delay.

Periwyn pledged not only her word, but the word of her family and her insignia as guarantee of payment once the task was complete. The priest accepted the pledge with the sober grace of a man who had not expected to be paid in anything so weighty.

Not wishing to be caught by the guards, they told him to meet them by the western gate. Then, before leaving the city entirely, they left a note in Durov's room — instructing him to meet them at the cult's facility.

---

**Durov, meanwhile**

Durov himself was, of course, still at the facility — but only briefly.

After melting down the last of the weapons and armor, he loaded everything onto Rimefang and left, though not before stopping to ask the gate guard for directions, if only to be certain of the road.

His stop was the same as always.

The Frostgate Hall.

Inside, Orgnar the innkeeper looked exhausted — though still, somehow, genuinely glad to see his most regular patron. He explained, while pouring, what had happened since the fires: the Silver Road had conducted a surprise inspection in the early hours of the morning. They owned the building and the land the inn stood upon, and given its reputation as the main stop for travelers and mercenaries, it had not taken a genius to guess why it had been searched.

It had still been rude.

And it had left Orgnar with the duty of cleaning up the entire place himself.

Durov ordered his usual sweet pork, paid double to cover Orgnar's trouble, and sat at one of the corner tables, eating in silence.

Later, Orgnar approached him — war axe in hand, of all things — for a quick and important chat.

He handed Durov a folded piece of paper.

Inside was the recipe for the Sweet Pork Durov had been ordering all this time. Orgnar explained, with the weary calm of a man who had made peace with something, that with everything going on in the city, the inn might not stand for much longer. He told Durov to keep sharing the recipe, to keep using it, so that it would go on living far past the inn itself — in kitchens and inns across every road, long after Frostgate Hall was only a memory.

As for the war axe?

He was joining the people in the market. Off to another protest. Taking it, he said, in case things got violent again.

They embraced.

_"May the gods watch over you, my friend,"_ Orgnar told him.

Durov promised to spread the recipe to every inn in every city — a small vow, and one of the truest he had ever made.

Then they parted, and Orgnar walked out of the inn he had kept for years, and did not look back.

Durov sat for a while longer in the emptying hall.

Then he found the note the others had left, gathered anything not already taken, took the cart and the horses, and left for the Tomb of the Oath-Breaker King.

---

**The Road to the Mesa**

He arrived just as the Vestige was leaving.

They left the cart outside, but put the horses inside the ruin with some hay, so they would not freeze in the night's cold, and then walked together toward the Altan Mesa.

Durov seized the opportunity.

He had questions, and he had waited long enough to ask them.

The road was long, and the Vestige, it turned out, was in a talking mood.

He spoke, first, of his age — one hundred and twelve years, which for a Dunmer was only middle age, a fact Durov found faintly absurd. Of how he had come to be the Vestige — though he corrected himself, gently, as though the distinction still mattered after all this time. He had not been *the* Vestige, only *a* vestige. Vestiges were made when a mortal's soul was replaced by that of a Daedra. His own had been taken in a ritual to Molag Bal — murdered by the necromancer Mannimarco, the King of Worms, his soul trapped in Coldharbour. He had escaped with the help of powers he did not fully name, and had fought for years to win his true soul back — succeeding, at last, and ending the Planemeld.

The process had left something behind, though. Something no one else in Tamriel possessed: immortal life, technically. As any Daedric vestige could, he rematerialized after his body was killed. He could not truly die.

It was also, he explained, the reason for his immense skill at so many things.

When one is not afraid of death or mistakes, one can spend a very long time learning from both.

The armor he wore — the malachite scales, the ebony skeleton, and the strange black leathers that disappeared briefly in the shadows — had been custom-made by the greatest craftsmen and women on the continent, paid for by wealth accumulated over many years in service to monarchs and noblemen.

He knew of Majorn the Ancient personally, having fought the vampire years ago while serving under the Ebonheart Pact. Majorn's brood, he said, would be formidable indeed, if they were truly behind the recent vampire activity in the Rift. And how did he know all that?

From Archmagus Mortgren.

They were old friends, he said — from the days when the Vestige had still been a mercenary working for High King Emeric. It was Mortgren who kept him informed of ongoings, and who had briefed him on the Fangs' encounter with Majorn's vampires.

Could the dead be brought back as he had been? Durov did not ask it directly, but the Vestige answered anyway, as though the question had been in the air between them.

Technically, yes — but only if the dead had suffered the same affliction he had. Theoretically, one could go to whatever plane of Oblivion held a stolen soul and retrieve it. But Oblivion, he noted dryly, was not exactly easy to escape from.

Then Durov asked the question that gave the Vestige pause.

Whether he ever tired of this life.

The old warrior said nothing for a moment.

Then he let down his guard — pulled back his mask and hood, and looked at Durov with the eyes of someone who had been doing this for far too long.

_"I do,"_ he said. _"Every day, I do. But tell me, Colovian... who would pick up the slack?"_

On the matter of morality — whether the world held more good or more evil, in the Vestige's long view — his answer surprised Durov.

He spoke of the little moments. The small kindnesses that filled people's lives no matter how bad things became. The kind word to a stranger. The shared meal. The hand offered in the snow. Those, he said, still remained, in every age, in every ruin, in every war. For it was not always about heroism.

It was about kindness.

And that happened far more often than not.

Before they parted ways for the night, Durov took off his helmet. The Vestige studied him a moment, and remarked that he'd have expected more beard.

He estimated Durov was somewhere under thirty.

---

**Nightfall, at the Altan Mesa**

The Fangs and the Vestige converged near the Altan Mesa as the last light failed, the ruin's brass doors just visible in the gloom ahead.

Durov, in a rare mood, decided to pull a prank.

He circled wide, crept up behind his companions, and sprung from the shadows with all the theatrical menace a Colovian in full plate could manage. Periwyn, to her credit, managed to spot him — but Onasha did not, and jumped badly as he appeared behind her, nearly loosing a spell at his head before recognition caught up with reflex.

The Vestige had vanished.

He left only a note: he would return once the mage was gone.

To make things easier for the others, Durov sought out the gate guard — the same one he had asked for directions earlier that day — and, finding the man exhausted, talked him into letting Durov take over the remainder of his shift.

And so the only sentry at the Altan Mesa that night was Durov.

The warded doors stood on an elevated walkway, perhaps four meters above the ground. Durov, tall and strong as he was, helped the others up by simply tossing them onto the ledge — one by one, like sacks of grain, while they swore at him for it. Everyone made it up safely.

When the priest examined the wards, he confessed they were more complex than he had anticipated. But he began undeterred. He set out a ritual circle with candles and chalk, planted his staff at the centre, knelt, and began to pray.

A beam of light erupted from the staff, striking the ward's centre —

And the ward began to dissolve.

But it was for naught.

The ward's magic was fighting back. Periwyn, unfamiliar with this kind of contested ritual, grabbed Onasha and stepped back. Just then, the ward's energy crept up the beam of light, reached the staff, and erupted violently — hurling the priest backward off the platform.

Durov caught him.

Barely.

The priest seemed alive, though unconscious.

Durov set him down gently, then — with the instinct of a man who had survived worse nights than this — rolled himself in the mud near the stables, giving himself an alibi for all the noise, and resumed his guard post as though nothing had happened, glaring disapprovingly at Periwyn the entire time.

Periwyn, try as she did, could only muster the look of absolute horror on her face. For the priest, the one that trusted her word, her status as a Knight of the Circle, the one she hired to do this... Laid motionless on the ground.

The Vestige appeared on the walkway moments later.

He looked at Periwyn, and offered only a single, quiet sentence:

_"We will talk about this later."_

Then he dissolved the second ward with a varnish of hagraven feather — the plan B that had, at the last moment, become plan A.

And then, at last, they walked in.
